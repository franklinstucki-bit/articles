Clients bring all kinds of harm into your office. Some of it came from a car crash. Some came from a church, a school, a youth program or a residential facility that was supposed to keep them safe. Your work is the healing. But sometimes a client's story also raises a legal question they don't know to ask.

This guide is for Washington licensed mental health counselors, marriage and family therapists, independent clinical social workers and psychologists. It covers when a civil referral may help, how to raise it without pressure, and how Washington's privilege, deadline and reporting rules fit together.

**The short answer:** Consider mentioning a civil attorney when a client's trauma was caused by someone else's wrongdoing or negligence, such as a crash, abuse by a person in authority, or harm in a troubled-teen program. A referral is optional for the client and separate from your mandated reporting duty. If a client later sues for emotional harm, expect their counseling records to be sought, so it helps to talk with them early about what that means.

## Signs a Client's Trauma May Be Actionable in Washington

You don't need to decide whether a client has a case. These are patterns where a short conversation with a lawyer can help a client understand their options.

### Crashes and other injuries

- Anxiety, panic or PTSD symptoms that started after a car, motorcycle, bike or pedestrian crash.
- A brain injury affecting mood, memory or concentration, or grief after a negligent death.

Washington's general deadline for injury lawsuits is three years (RCW 4.16.080).

### Institutional abuse

- Sexual abuse by a teacher, coach, clergy member, youth leader, foster parent or staff member.
- Signs that an institution knew or should have known and didn't act: prior complaints, transfers, ignored reports, or a culture of silence.
- Abuse disclosed years later, often in adulthood. Delayed disclosure is common and does not by itself end a client's options.

### Troubled-teen and residential programs

- Clients who were sent to wilderness programs, therapeutic boarding schools or residential treatment, in Washington or out of state.
- Reports of restraint, isolation, food or sleep deprivation, forced labor, humiliation-based "therapy," or cut-off contact with family.
- Sexual abuse by staff or peers that the program didn't prevent.

## How to Raise a Legal Referral Without Pressure

Some clients have spent years being told what to do by people with power over them. The referral conversation should feel like the opposite of that.

- **Follow the client's lead.** Mention it when the client raises fairness, accountability or money stress, not in the middle of processing.
- **Offer, don't recommend an outcome.** "Some people in your situation talk to a lawyer just to understand their options. Would that be useful to know more about?"
- **Make no promises.** Avoid predicting what a case is worth or whether it would succeed.
- **Keep it optional and revisitable.** "No" is a full answer, and the client can bring it up later.
- **Respect the timing.** Some clients will want to wait until they're more stable, as long as they know deadlines exist.

The client can make the call themselves. You don't need to share anything.

## Washington Privilege and What Happens When a Client Files Suit

### Who is covered

Under RCW 5.60.060(9), a mental health counselor, independent clinical social worker or marriage and family therapist licensed under chapter 18.225 RCW cannot be compelled to disclose information a client shared that was necessary to provide professional services. The statute lists exceptions, including the client's written authorization, a client bringing charges against the counselor, a Department of Health subpoena related to a complaint, disclosures required under the child and vulnerable adult abuse reporting chapters, and imminent danger to health or safety.

For psychologists and licensed psychological associates, RCW 18.83.110 places client communications on the same footing as attorney-client communications, subject to limits in RCW 71.05.217.

(Chapter 5.62 RCW, sometimes cited in this context, covers registered nurses, not counselors.)

### Waiver when the client sues

For physicians, RCW 5.60.060(4)(b) provides that a person who files a personal injury or wrongful death action waives the physician-patient privilege 90 days after filing. The counselor privilege in subsection (9) does not contain the same built-in rule. Even so, when a client sues and claims emotional or psychological harm, the defense will usually seek counseling records, and a court may find the privilege waived to the extent the client has put their mental health at issue. The scope of what gets disclosed is often negotiated or decided by the court.

### How records are requested

Chapter 70.02 RCW governs health care information. Under RCW 70.02.060, an attorney seeking a provider's records by discovery request or subpoena must first give notice to both the provider and the patient, with at least 14 days for the patient to seek a protective order. Producing records under that section does not by itself waive any privilege. If you receive a request, it's reasonable to confirm the notice was given and talk with your own counsel or professional liability carrier.

For clients considering a claim, it helps to know early that therapy notes may be requested.

## Washington Childhood Sexual Abuse Deadlines (RCW 4.16.340)

Washington amended its childhood sexual abuse statute in 2024 (2024 c 253). Under RCW 4.16.340 as amended:

- **Abuse on or after June 6, 2024:** there is no time limit for claims based on intentional conduct for injury from childhood sexual abuse.
- **Abuse before June 6, 2024:** a claim must be filed within the latest of three years from the act, three years from when the survivor discovered or reasonably should have discovered that the act caused the injury, or three years from when the survivor discovered that the act caused the injury the claim is based on. The time is tolled until age 18.

The discovery rules matter. A survivor who only recently connected their depression, substance use or relationship struggles to the abuse may still be within the time allowed. How these rules apply to claims against the institution that employed or housed the abuser can be a separate legal question, so an early consultation helps.

## Mandated Reporting Is a Separate Duty

A civil referral and a mandated report are different things, and one never replaces the other.

RCW 26.44.030 lists mandated reporters of child abuse and neglect, including psychologists and social service counselors. When a mandated reporter has reasonable cause to believe a child has suffered abuse or neglect, the report must be made at the first opportunity and no later than 48 hours after forming that belief, to law enforcement or the state department as provided in RCW 26.44.040. The statute was amended in 2024 and 2025, so it's worth reviewing current text and your licensing board's guidance.

Whether a report is required when an adult client discloses past childhood abuse depends on the statute's terms and the facts, including whether a child may currently be at risk. A referral to a civil attorney has no effect on that duty either way.

## FAQ

### When should a Washington therapist refer a client to a lawyer?
Answer: Consider mentioning a civil attorney when a client's trauma was caused by someone else's wrongdoing or negligence, such as a crash, sexual abuse by a person in authority, or harm in a residential or troubled-teen program. The referral should be optional and pressure-free. It is separate from any mandated reporting duty.

### Is therapy privileged in Washington if my client files a lawsuit?
Answer: Washington protects communications with licensed mental health counselors, LICSWs and LMFTs under RCW 5.60.060(9), and with psychologists under RCW 18.83.110. When a client sues and claims emotional or psychological harm, the defense will usually seek counseling records, and a court may find the privilege waived to the extent the client put their mental health at issue. Clients should know this before deciding to file.

### What is the statute of limitations for childhood sexual abuse in Washington?
Answer: Under RCW 4.16.340, as amended in 2024, there is no time limit for claims based on childhood sexual abuse occurring on or after June 6, 2024. For earlier abuse, the claim must be filed within the latest of several three-year periods, including three years from when the survivor discovered the abuse caused the injury, and the time is tolled until age 18.

### Does referring a client to an attorney replace mandated reporting in Washington?
Answer: No. Mandated reporting under RCW 26.44.030 is a separate legal duty. Psychologists and social service counselors are listed as mandated reporters, and reports must be made at the first opportunity and within 48 hours of forming reasonable cause to believe a child has been abused or neglected.

### What should I do if I get a subpoena for therapy records in Washington?
Answer: Under RCW 70.02.060, an attorney must give notice to both the provider and the patient, with at least 14 days to seek a protective order, before serving a discovery request or subpoena for health care information. Check that notice was given, and consider contacting your own counsel or professional liability carrier before producing records.
