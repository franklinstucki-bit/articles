You were riding the way you always do. Then a driver turned left across your lane, drifted into you on I-5, or pulled out from a side street without looking. Now you're dealing with injuries, a damaged bike, and an insurance adjuster who seems to have already decided what happened.

Riders in Washington often run into the same problems after a crash: assumptions about who was at fault, confusion about which insurance pays, and questions about the helmet. This guide walks through each one in plain English.

**The short answer:** In Washington, you can still recover damages after a motorcycle crash even if you were partly at fault, because the state uses pure comparative fault. Washington PIP usually does not cover riders on a motorcycle, so your own underinsured motorist (UIM) coverage and the at-fault driver's liability insurance matter more. The general deadline to file an injury lawsuit is three years.

## Bias Against Motorcycle Riders in Washington Claims

Many people, including some adjusters and jurors, carry a picture of riders as reckless. That picture shows up in small ways: an adjuster who asks how fast you were going before asking what the driver did, or a police report that leans on the driver's version because you were in the ambulance.

Most car-versus-motorcycle crashes involve a driver who did not see the rider. A fair claim starts by setting the bias aside and looking at the evidence: sight lines, lane position, skid marks, vehicle damage, video and witnesses. Riders who know the bias exists can prepare for it instead of being surprised by it.

## Washington's Motorcycle Helmet Law and Your Injury Claim

Washington requires both operators and passengers on a motorcycle, motor-driven cycle or moped to wear a motorcycle helmet on public roads, with the chin or neck strap fastened while the bike is moving (RCW 46.37.530). The statute defines a qualifying helmet as one with a hard outer shell, padding, a retention strap and a manufacturer certification that it meets U.S. Department of Transportation standards. The same statute also covers mirrors, eye protection and a ban on carrying children under five.

### What if you weren't wearing a helmet?

Not wearing a helmet does not cause a crash, and it does not take away your right to bring a claim. But the other side may argue that some of your injuries, usually head injuries, would have been less serious with a helmet, and that your damages should be reduced for that reason. Whether and how that argument is allowed depends on the facts, the medical evidence and the court's rulings. It generally has little to do with a broken leg, a shoulder injury or road rash.

If you weren't wearing a helmet, or your helmet didn't meet the DOT standard, tell your lawyer early so it can be dealt with honestly rather than discovered later.

## Pure Comparative Fault in Washington State

Washington follows pure comparative fault. Under RCW 4.22.005, your own share of fault reduces your damages proportionally, but it does not bar recovery.

In practice, that means an insurer saying "you were partly at fault" is the start of a conversation, not the end of your claim. If a driver turned in front of you and the insurer argues you were going a little fast, the question becomes how fault should be divided, not whether you get anything at all.

## Washington PIP Usually Doesn't Cover Motorcycle Riders

In a Washington car crash, personal injury protection (PIP) often pays early medical bills and some lost wages regardless of fault. Riders are often surprised to learn it usually isn't there for them.

Here's why. Washington's PIP statutes are built around an "automobile," which RCW 48.22.005 defines by reference to a "passenger car." The definition of passenger car in RCW 46.04.382 expressly excludes motorcycles and motor-driven cycles. The PIP offer and exception sections (RCW 48.22.085 and 48.22.090) work within that "automobile" framework; the motorcycle gap comes from the definitions rather than a separate listed exclusion. So PIP that comes with a car policy generally does not follow you onto your bike.

What that means for you:

- Your health insurance may be the main source of payment for treatment early on.
- Some motorcycle policies offer optional medical payments coverage. Check your declarations page.
- Keep every bill and explanation of benefits. These become part of the damages in a claim against the at-fault driver.

## Underinsured Motorist (UIM) Coverage for Washington Riders

Underinsured motorist coverage pays when the at-fault driver has no insurance or not enough to cover your losses. It can also apply in some hit-and-run situations.

RCW 48.22.030 requires insurers to include UIM coverage on Washington auto policies unless you reject it in writing, but motorcycles and motor-driven cycles are carved out of that requirement. Insurers that write motorcycle coverage must give you information about UIM and allow you to reject it for your motorcycle in writing.

Points worth checking:

- **Your motorcycle policy.** Does it include UIM, and at what limits?
- **Your car policy.** Coverage for a vehicle you own that isn't insured under that policy can be excluded, so your car's UIM may not reach you on your bike. The policy language controls.
- **Phantom vehicles.** If a car ran you off the road without touching you, Washington UIM law treats it as a "phantom vehicle" only if the facts are backed by evidence beyond your own word and the crash was reported to police within 72 hours.

## The Washington Deadline: Three Years

Washington's general deadline to file a lawsuit for personal injury is three years (RCW 4.16.080(2)). Some claims have earlier steps. Claims against a city, county or the State, for example over a road defect, require a formal claim form and a waiting period before suit. Insurance policies also have their own notice and cooperation requirements.

Three years goes faster than people expect, especially while you're still treating. Talking to a lawyer early keeps your options open.

## Evidence and Gear After a Washington Motorcycle Crash

The physical evidence from a motorcycle crash tells a story, and it disappears quickly.

- **Keep your gear.** Don't throw away your helmet, jacket, gloves, pants or boots, even if they're ruined. Abrasion, impact marks and tears can show how the crash happened and how hard you hit.
- **Don't repair the bike yet.** Photograph it from every angle, and ask before the insurer or a shop disposes of it.
- **Photos of the scene.** Lane markings, intersection layout, sight-line obstructions, debris and skid marks.
- **Video.** Helmet cams and dash cams matter. Nearby businesses and traffic cameras often overwrite footage within days or weeks, so ask quickly.
- **Witnesses.** Names and phone numbers, even of people who only heard the impact.
- **Medical records and a simple journal.** How you feel, what you can't do, and missed work.
- **Be careful with recorded statements.** You can be polite with the other driver's insurer without giving a recorded statement on the first call.

## FAQ

### Is Washington a no-fault state for motorcycle accidents?
Answer: No. Washington is a fault-based state, so the driver who caused the crash and their insurer are responsible for the harm. Washington also uses pure comparative fault, which means your damages can be reduced by your share of fault but not eliminated.

### Can I still get compensation in Washington if I wasn't wearing a helmet?
Answer: Yes, not wearing a helmet does not bar a claim in Washington. The other side may argue that some injuries, usually head injuries, would have been less serious with a helmet. How much that matters depends on the facts, the medical evidence and the court.

### Does PIP cover motorcycle accidents in Washington State?
Answer: Usually not. Washington's PIP law is tied to "automobiles," defined as passenger cars, and the passenger car definition excludes motorcycles. Riders often rely on health insurance, optional medical payments coverage on their motorcycle policy, and the at-fault driver's liability coverage.

### Do I have UIM coverage on my motorcycle in Washington?
Answer: Maybe. Washington requires UIM on auto policies unless rejected in writing, but motorcycles are excluded from that requirement, and riders can reject UIM on a motorcycle policy in writing. Check your motorcycle policy's declarations page to see whether you have it and at what limits.

### How long do I have to file a motorcycle accident lawsuit in Washington?
Answer: The general deadline for a personal injury lawsuit in Washington is three years under RCW 4.16.080. Claims involving a government entity have extra steps, including a formal claim form and a waiting period, so it's wise to talk with a lawyer early.

### What should I do with my helmet and gear after a crash?
Answer: Keep everything, including a damaged helmet, jacket, gloves and boots. Gear can show how the crash happened and the forces involved. Photograph your bike before repairs and ask before anyone disposes of it.
