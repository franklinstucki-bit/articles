From October through spring, western Washington settles into its long gray season. Roads stay wet for weeks, daylight is short, and ferry lines stretch back from the terminals in Anacortes, Friday Harbor, Seattle and Kingston. Most trips are uneventful. But when a crash happens in the rain or in a ferry lane, the questions that follow can be different from an ordinary fender-bender.

**The short answer:** Washington drivers must keep a safe following distance and slow down for weather and road conditions, so wet roads don't excuse a rear-end or loss-of-control crash. Crashes in ferry holding lanes are usually handled like any other collision between drivers. If the State of Washington or a local government may be responsible, you generally must file a formal tort claim and wait 60 days before filing suit.

## Rain-Season Crashes in Western Washington

Rain changes how a car stops and steers. The first rain after a dry stretch lifts oil to the road surface. Standing water collects in ruts on older highways. Leaves pile up in gutters and cover lane markings. And on islands and rural roads, there may be no streetlights at all by 5 p.m. in December.

### Following distance on wet roads

Washington law says a driver may not follow another vehicle more closely than is "reasonable and prudent," with regard for speed, traffic and the condition of the highway (RCW 46.61.145). Washington's basic speed rule also requires drivers to slow down when special hazards exist because of weather or highway conditions (RCW 46.61.400).

In plain terms, the posted speed limit is a ceiling, not a target. A driver who rear-ends you on a wet road usually can't blame the rain, because the law expects drivers to leave more room when it's wet.

### Hydroplaning

Hydroplaning happens when a layer of water builds up between the tires and the road, and the car briefly loses contact with the pavement. Speed, worn tire tread and standing water all make it more likely.

A driver who hydroplanes into another lane may argue it was unavoidable. Often the better question is why the car was going fast enough, or on tires worn enough, to lose grip. Sometimes the answer also points to the road itself, such as poor drainage or a long-standing pooling problem, which may raise a claim against whoever maintains that road.

## Ferry Holding-Lane Crashes: Anacortes, the San Juan Islands and Puget Sound

Ferry holding lanes have their own rhythm. Cars sit for an hour or more, then everyone starts moving at once. People get out to stretch, walk dogs or grab coffee. Drivers merge from side lanes and creep forward in the dark and rain. On the vessel, crew members direct cars into tight rows, and passengers walk between vehicles to reach the stairs.

Common situations include:

- **Low-speed rear-end collisions** when a line starts moving or a driver looks down at a phone.
- **Pedestrians struck** in the holding area or on the car deck.
- **Loading and unloading crashes**, where a driver misreads a crew signal or another driver moves early.
- **Falls** on wet ramps, stairs or decks.

A low-speed crash is not the same as a harmless one. Neck and back injuries are common in rear-end impacts, especially when you're stopped and not braced.

Most holding-lane crashes are claims against the other driver's insurer, handled like any Washington car crash. When the conduct of ferry crew, the design or condition of a terminal, or a hazard on the vessel is involved, a claim may also run against the State of Washington, because Washington State Ferries is operated by the state.

### What to do after a ferry-lane crash

- Photograph both vehicles, the lane number and the terminal signage.
- Get the other driver's name, insurance and plate.
- Tell terminal staff or the crew, and ask whether an incident report was made.
- Ask about terminal camera footage quickly. Video is often kept only for a limited time.
- Get names of any crew members or passengers who saw it.
- If you're hurt on an island, don't wait to see a doctor until you're back on the mainland.

## Claims Against the State of Washington

When the State or one of its employees or agencies may be at fault, Washington law adds steps before you can file a lawsuit.

### The standard tort claim form

Under RCW 4.92.100, a claim against the State must be presented to the Office of Risk Management within the Department of Enterprise Services, using the State's standard tort claim form. The form asks for, among other things, a description of what happened and the injury, the time and place, the people involved, the amount of damages claimed, and your residence when the claim arose and when you file. It must be signed by you, a Washington-licensed attorney, a holder of a written power of attorney, or a court-approved guardian. The statute says the requirements are to be liberally construed and substantial compliance is enough, but it's still wise to get it right the first time.

The Department of Enterprise Services accepts claims online, by fax, by mail, or in person by appointment.

### The 60-day waiting period

Under RCW 4.92.110, you cannot file a lawsuit against the State until 60 calendar days after the claim is presented. The statute of limitations is paused during those 60 days, and a suit filed within five court days after the waiting period ends is treated as timely for limitations purposes.

### A note on ferry claims

There is also an older ferry-specific statute, RCW 47.60.250, which describes a verified claim to the department within 120 days. How it fits with the general State claim process is a question to raise with a lawyer early. The safe course is to treat the shorter window as real and act well before it.

## Claims Against a Washington City, County or Other Local Government

Road maintenance on island and rural roads is often handled by the county, and city streets by the city or town. Claims against these local governments follow RCW 4.96.020:

- Each local government must appoint an agent to receive claims, and that agent's identity and address are recorded with the county auditor.
- The claim must be presented to that agent within the time allowed for filing suit, generally using the standard tort claim form or the entity's own compliant form.
- No lawsuit may be filed until 60 calendar days after the claim is presented, and the statute of limitations is paused during that time.

Washington's general deadline for a personal injury lawsuit is three years (RCW 4.16.080), but the claim-form step has to happen inside that time, and the 60-day wait has to be planned for.

## FAQ

### Who is at fault for a rear-end crash in the rain in Washington?
Answer: Usually the driver who rear-ends another car. Washington law requires drivers to keep a reasonable and prudent following distance and to slow down for weather and road conditions. Rain is a reason to leave more room, not an excuse for a collision.

### Can I make a claim if someone hits me in a Washington ferry line?
Answer: Yes. Most ferry holding-lane crashes are handled as a claim against the other driver's insurance, like any Washington car crash. If ferry crew conduct or a terminal hazard played a role, a claim against the State of Washington may also be possible.

### How do I file a claim against Washington State Ferries or the State of Washington?
Answer: Claims against the State generally go to the Office of Risk Management at the Department of Enterprise Services on the State's standard tort claim form, under RCW 4.92.100. You must then wait 60 days before filing a lawsuit. An older ferry statute, RCW 47.60.250, also describes a 120-day claim window, so act early.

### How long do I have to wait before suing the State of Washington?
Answer: Sixty calendar days after your tort claim is presented, under RCW 4.92.110. The statute of limitations is paused during that period. Local government claims under RCW 4.96.020 have a similar 60-day wait.

### Can I sue a Washington county for a dangerous road?
Answer: Possibly, if the county's maintenance or design of the road contributed to the crash. You first have to present a tort claim to the county's designated agent under RCW 4.96.020 and wait 60 days. These claims depend heavily on the facts and the evidence of the road condition.

### Is hydroplaning considered an accident nobody is responsible for?
Answer: Not necessarily. Speed, worn tires and following distance all affect whether a car hydroplanes, and drivers are expected to adjust for wet roads. In some cases, poor road drainage may also point to responsibility by whoever maintains the road.
