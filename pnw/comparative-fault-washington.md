**Quick answer:** Washington follows a pure comparative fault rule, so being partly at fault for a crash reduces what you can recover but does not by itself bar your claim. Your share of fault is subtracted from the total, and the rest of the claim stays on the table.

The rain had been falling since dawn. Traffic on I-405 near Bellevue went from sixty to nothing in a few hundred feet. You braked late, the car ahead braked later, and the truck behind you never braked at all.

Afterward, the adjuster asks a quiet question: weren't you going a little fast for the conditions? That question is about comparative fault. It is worth understanding before you answer it.

## What does "pure comparative fault" mean in Washington?

Washington's rule is written in [RCW 4.22.005](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=4.22.005). In a fault-based injury case, any fault chargeable to the injured person diminishes the award proportionately but does not bar recovery.

In plain terms, a jury or an insurer looks at everyone involved and assigns each a percentage. Your recovery is reduced by your own percentage. Nothing more.

Some states cut people off entirely once they cross 50 or 51 percent. Washington does not use that kind of cutoff. That is what the word "pure" signals.

## How is fault actually divided?

There is no formula printed on a chart. Fault is decided from evidence: photos, dash-cam and doorbell video, the police report, witness statements, vehicle damage, and sometimes data pulled from the cars themselves.

Washington's rules of the road shape the argument. A driver must keep a speed that is "reasonable and prudent under the conditions" under [RCW 46.61.400](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=46.61.400), and must not follow too closely under [RCW 46.61.145](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=46.61.145). In a Northwest winter, those two rules carry a lot of weight.

A simple illustration, with round numbers only: if a claim is valued at 100 units and you are found 20 percent at fault, the recovery would be 80 units. The math is easy. The fight is over the percentage.

## Why do insurers raise comparative fault so often?

Because every point of fault they can assign to you is money they keep. It is a normal part of the process, not a sign your claim is weak.

Common arguments we see in Washington crashes include:

- Speed on wet pavement on I-5, I-90 or SR-520
- Following distance in stop-and-go traffic near Seattle, Tacoma or Everett
- Riding position or lane choice on a motorcycle
- A pedestrian or rider "should have seen" the vehicle in low light
- Not slowing for black ice on a shaded curve or a bridge deck

## Does not wearing a seat belt count against me?

Washington has a specific rule here. Under [RCW 46.61.688(6)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=46.61.688), failure to wear a safety belt is not admissible as evidence of negligence in a civil action. Insurers sometimes raise it anyway in conversation. That statute is worth knowing.

## What should I avoid saying after a crash?

You do not need to be guarded with your own doctor or your family. You do want to be careful with the other driver's insurer.

- Avoid guessing at your speed or distances. "I don't know" is an honest answer.
- Avoid apologizing at the scene. It can be read as an admission, even when you meant it as courtesy.
- Talk to a lawyer before giving the other driver's insurer a recorded statement; your own insurer may require cooperation under your policy.

## Local specifics: where shared-fault arguments show up

The Northwest has its own patterns. Merges at the I-5 and I-405 interchange near Lynnwood. Ferry traffic unloading in waves onto SR-20 in Anacortes. Wildlife on rural two-lane roads near Spokane and Bellingham. Afternoon glare off wet pavement on SR-520 when the clouds finally break.

In each, the other side may argue you could have done something differently. Good evidence, gathered early, is usually what answers that argument.

## FAQ

### Can I still recover if I was mostly at fault?

Under Washington's pure comparative fault rule, recovery is reduced by your share of fault rather than barred. Whether a claim is worth pursuing at that point depends on the facts and the coverage involved.

### Who decides my percentage of fault?

In a settlement, it is negotiated between the parties and insurers. If the case goes to trial, the jury decides.

### Does the police report decide fault?

No. A police report is important evidence, but it is not the final word on civil fault.
