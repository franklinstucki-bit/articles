Therapists hear things no one else hears. Sometimes a client's account of sexual assault or abuse includes a second story: what the school, program or organization did after it learned. That second story is often where legal questions live.

This note is written for clinicians who treat Utah survivors, whether you practice in Utah or see clients who moved from Utah to Nevada, Washington or elsewhere. It explains what to listen for, why some Utah survivors hesitate to come forward, and how to give a client the option of a confidential legal consult while keeping the decision entirely theirs.

## Listen for the institution, not just the incident

A therapist's focus is the client's healing. A lawyer's first question is often different: who was responsible for keeping this person safe, and what did they do? You can help simply by noticing when the institution is part of the story.

Themes worth noting:

- **Someone in charge knew.** A teacher, coach, youth leader, residential staff member or administrator was told or saw warning signs.
- **The setting was controlled.** Campus housing, a residential treatment or wilderness program, a school, or an organized church or youth activity.
- **Reporting backfired.** The client faced discipline, an inquiry into their own conduct, loss of housing, a scholarship or a job, or pressure to stay quiet.
- **Information traveled.** Something the client shared in confidence reached a school office, employer or religious leader without their agreement.
- **Paperwork is unclear.** The client signed statements or releases under pressure, or cannot get their records.

You do not need to evaluate any of this. Noticing it is enough.

## Why Utah survivors may stay quiet

Utah has several large universities and many faith-connected communities. For some clients, the fear of institutional consequences is rooted in public events.

In 2016, Brigham Young University students said that reporting sexual assault had led to Honor Code investigations. BYU's advisory council found that the Title IX office had sometimes shared information with the Honor Code Office. In October 2016, BYU accepted all 23 of the council's recommendations, including an amnesty clause stating that "being a victim of sexual misconduct is never a violation of the Honor Code," a Title IX office located apart from the Honor Code Office, and limits on sharing a complainant's information without consent.

A later state Peace Officer Standards and Training investigation found that a former BYU police lieutenant had shared information from other agencies' police reports with BYU offices, including the Honor Code Office, from 2014 to 2016. The state moved to decertify BYU's police department in 2019. An administrative judge dismissed that effort in early 2021.

Federal review has reached other Utah campuses too. The U.S. Department of Justice opened a Title IX compliance review of Utah State University in 2017 and, in 2020, announced a resolution agreement after concluding the university had not complied with Title IX.

Rules have changed since then. But a client's fear can outlast a policy change, and it deserves a respectful, accurate answer.

## When the first counselor was a faith-based referral

Some clients first received help through Family Services, a welfare resource of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints formerly called LDS Family Services. Its published description says a bishop's referral is required, counseling is "typically up to 12 sessions," and its purpose includes helping members in "overcoming obstacles to making and keeping sacred covenants."

That is a different starting point from secular codes of ethics, which center client self-determination, informed consent and confidentiality. It may be useful to ask, without judgment:

- Who referred the client, and what was the referral for?
- What did the client share with ecclesiastical leaders?
- Did they consent to that information being shared, and with whom?

## A word on your own duties

Utah law requires immediate reporting of suspected child abuse or neglect (Utah Code 80-2-602) and of suspected abuse, neglect or exploitation of a vulnerable adult (Utah Code 26B-6-205). Utah's duty-to-warn statute for therapists (Utah Code 78B-3-502) applies when a client communicates an actual threat of physical violence against a clearly or reasonably identifiable victim.

If you practice in another state, that state's rules govern your duties. In every case, check with your own counsel or licensing board.

## Timing is the quiet risk

Filing deadlines for abuse-related claims vary by claim, by defendant and by state, and Utah's legislature has revisited its sexual-abuse limitation laws in recent sessions. Some deadlines are short. Claims involving public entities, such as state universities, generally require a formal notice of claim first.

That is why an early, no-commitment conversation can matter. A client who learns their options early keeps more of them.

## FAQ

### Is this only for clients who live in Utah?

No. Some survivors leave Utah after school or a youth program. Where the harm happened and who was involved usually matter more than where the client lives now.

### What should I say if my client asks whether they have a case?

It is fine to say you don't know and that a lawyer can answer that confidentially. Avoid predicting outcomes.

### Does suggesting a consult mean I think my client should sue?

No. A consult is information. Many people talk with a lawyer and decide not to take any action.

### Can I speak with the lawyer on my client's behalf?

Only with the client's written authorization. Otherwise, give the client the contact information and let them decide.

### My client says it happened too long ago. Should I tell them it's too late?

No. Deadlines depend on the facts and have changed over time. Let a lawyer assess it.
