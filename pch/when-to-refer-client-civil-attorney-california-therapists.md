Some clients arrive carrying two stories. One is about what happened to them as teenagers in a residential program, boarding school, wilderness program or other institution. The other is about what the institution did afterward: ignored a report, punished the person who spoke up, lost the records, or told the family everything was fine.

That second story can matter legally. Many clients do not know that, and some have never asked anyone.

This guide is for California mental health professionals, including LMFTs, LCSWs, LPCCs and psychologists, who treat adults who were sent to troubled-teen programs as minors or who experienced abuse inside an institution. It is not about turning therapy into case-building. It is about recognizing when a confidential conversation with a lawyer might be useful to the client, and offering that option in a way that respects their choice.

## Why the question comes up in therapy

Adults who went through troubled-teen programs often describe isolation, restricted contact with family, restraint, punishment for reporting, or staff who knew and did nothing. Clients who were harmed in schools, churches, youth programs or care facilities describe similar patterns.

Researchers have a name for the harm that comes from an institution's response: institutional betrayal. Smith and Freyd described how trusted institutions can harm the people who depend on them, both by causing harm and by failing to respond appropriately to it. Many clients describe that second layer as the part that still hurts most.

You do not need to decide whether there is a case. That is the lawyer's job. Your role is simply to notice when the client's story includes facts a lawyer would want to hear about, and to let the client know the option exists.

## Signs a trauma may be legally actionable

None of these signs means a client has a claim. Each is a reason the client might benefit from a confidential case review.

### Institutional failure

- The client says staff, administrators, clergy or other leaders knew or suspected abuse and did not act.
- The person who caused the harm had prior complaints the institution knew about.
- The harm happened in a setting the institution controlled, such as a residential facility, transport, a school campus or an organized activity.
- The client was placed as a minor in a program that limited phone calls, read or censored letters, or punished reporting.

### Retaliation after reporting

- The client reported and then faced punishment, isolation, loss of privileges, or a longer stay.
- The client was told to stay quiet, sign something, or handle it "internally."
- Family members were told the client was lying or manipulating, and the report went nowhere.

### Records problems

- The client has tried to get their own program, school or treatment records and been refused or ignored.
- Records seem incomplete, altered, or inconsistent with what the client remembers.
- The client is worried records will disappear as a program closes or changes names.

### A program, school or organization was involved

- A company, school, church, licensed facility, or transport service was part of the story.
- The client was sent across state lines. That can raise questions about where a claim belongs. Frank Stucki, Esq., MBA handles California matters directly and works with local co-counsel licensed in other states.

### The client is asking legal questions

Sometimes the clearest sign is the client's own words: "Is it too late?" "Could they get in trouble?" "Can I find out what they wrote about me?" Those are legal questions. A lawyer can answer them. Deadlines can be complex and depend on the facts, so it is better for a client to ask than to assume.

## How to raise the option without pressure

A referral should feel like information, not a recommendation to sue. Many survivors were controlled by adults who made decisions for them. A suggestion that sounds like an instruction can echo that.

### Ground the conversation in client autonomy

Both major professional codes support this approach. The CAMFT Code of Ethics (revised effective December 6, 2025) directs therapists to respect clients' choices and their right to make decisions (Standard 1.9), and to give clients adequate information in clear, understandable language so they can make meaningful decisions (Standard 3.1). The APA Ethics Code's General Principle E recognizes people's rights to privacy, confidentiality and self-determination, and Standard 3.10 addresses informed consent in language the person can understand.

In practice, that might sound like:

- "Some of what you've described, especially how the program responded, is the kind of thing people sometimes talk to a lawyer about. You don't have to do anything with that. I just want you to know it's an option."
- "If you ever want to find out whether you have any legal options, a confidential consult doesn't commit you to anything."
- "Whatever you decide, we can keep working on what matters to you here."

### Let the client set the pace

- Offer the information once, clearly, and then follow the client's lead.
- Normalize ambivalence. Wanting answers and not wanting a lawsuit can both be true.
- Explore what the client imagines a legal process would mean, including fears about being disbelieved or exposed.
- Make clear that therapy does not depend on the choice they make.

### Keep the roles separate

Your job stays clinical. You are not gathering evidence, and the client does not need to tell you more than they want to. If the client consults a lawyer, the lawyer will gather facts directly.

## Confidentiality basics in California

### The psychotherapist-patient privilege

California Evidence Code section 1014 gives the patient a privilege to refuse to disclose, and to prevent others from disclosing, confidential communications between patient and psychotherapist. The privilege can be claimed by the patient as holder, by a person the holder authorizes, or by the psychotherapist who was treating the patient at the time of the communication (with limits stated in the statute).

### The patient-litigant exception

Evidence Code section 1016 provides that there is no privilege for a communication relevant to an issue concerning the patient's mental or emotional condition if the patient tenders that issue, for example by claiming emotional injury in a lawsuit. The exception reaches communications that are relevant to that issue, so the scope can become a disputed question the attorneys and, if needed, a judge sort out.

This is one reason a client should talk with a lawyer before filing anything. The client deserves to understand, in advance, what parts of their mental health history might become part of a case.

### The CMIA

California's Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (Civil Code section 56 and following) generally bars a health care provider from disclosing medical information without the patient's authorization, subject to listed exceptions. One of those exceptions requires disclosure when compelled by a court order. Section 56.11 sets out what a valid authorization must contain. For practical purposes: do not share records or details with a lawyer, including the client's own lawyer, without a signed authorization from the client.

## Mandated reporting is a separate duty

A legal referral and a mandated report are two different things.

California Penal Code section 11166 requires a mandated reporter to report when, in their professional capacity, they know of or observe a child whom they know or reasonably suspect has been the victim of child abuse or neglect. The initial report goes by telephone immediately or as soon as practicably possible, with a written follow-up report within 36 hours.

The statute is written around a child. An adult's disclosure of abuse they experienced as a child generally does not, by itself, trigger a report. But if what you hear suggests a child is currently at risk, for example that the same person still works with minors, the analysis may change. Confirm your obligations with your own counsel, your professional association's legal and ethics resources, or your licensing board. A lawyer representing your client cannot advise you on your reporting duty.

## No referral fees

No referral fees are paid. California Rule of Professional Conduct 7.2(b) generally prohibits a lawyer from compensating, promising or giving anything of value to a person for recommending or securing the lawyer's services, with narrow listed exceptions such as paying for permitted advertising. The CAMFT Code of Ethics (Standard 12.1) likewise says therapists do not offer or accept payment for referrals, in money or otherwise.

A referral should rest on one thing: whether the client wants information about their options.

## FAQ

### Do I have to tell the lawyer anything about my client?

No. A client can contact a lawyer on their own. If the client later wants you to share information, that should happen only with a signed authorization.

### Will a consult commit my client to a lawsuit?

No. A confidential case review is a conversation. The client decides whether to take any next step.

### What if my client was in a program outside California?

They can still ask. Where a claim belongs depends on the facts. Cases in other states are handled with local co-counsel licensed there. For clients connected to Utah programs, see the [Utah therapist referral guide](https://slcsurvivorlaw.com/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

### Could my therapy records become part of a case?

They might, if the client claims emotional injury. Evidence Code section 1016 is the main reason. That is a question for the client to discuss with a lawyer before deciding anything.
