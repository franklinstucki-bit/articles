Maybe your son or daughter is in a wilderness program in another state. Maybe it is a residential treatment center, a therapeutic boarding school or a youth ranch. You made the decision because you were worried and wanted help. Now something feels off, and you are not sure whether to trust that feeling.

This article is for parents in Los Angeles, the Westside, Beverly Hills, Calabasas, the Valley, the South Bay, Orange County and anywhere else in Southern California whose child is in a program right now. It covers warning signs, practical steps, and when it may make sense to talk with a lawyer.

**Safety comes first.** If you believe your child is in immediate danger, call 911 or the local police where the program is located. You can also contact the child abuse hotline in that state. In Utah, the Division of Child and Family Services hotline is 1-855-323-DCFS (3237). Then come back to the rest of this list.

## Warning signs worth taking seriously

Not every hard moment means something is wrong. But certain patterns deserve a closer look.

### Restricted or monitored communication

- You can only talk with your child on scheduled calls, and staff are always on the line.
- Calls are cut short when your child starts to describe a problem.
- You were told not to "believe manipulation" or to discount what your child says about the program.

Some states have rules on this. Utah, for example, passed a law in 2021 (S.B. 127) requiring licensed congregate care programs to facilitate weekly confidential communication between a child and the child's parents, when not otherwise prohibited by law, and barring programs from using family contact as an incentive for behavior. If your child is in a Utah program and is not getting that kind of contact, ask why.

### Staff-filtered letters

- Letters from your child read like they were written for an audience.
- Your child hints that letters are read, edited or rewritten before you see them.

### "Levels" or phase systems

Many programs use levels or phases that control privileges such as phone calls, visits, clothing or time outside. Ask how a child moves up or down, who decides, and whether contact with family is ever tied to a level. A system where your child's access to you depends on staff approval is worth questioning.

### Restraint and seclusion

- Your child mentions being held down, "escorted," placed in a room alone, or isolated from peers.
- You notice bruises, scrapes or injuries that are explained vaguely.

Ask the program for its written policy on restraint and seclusion, and ask whether any have been used on your child. In Utah, S.B. 127 limited seclusion to immediate safety needs and barred its use for coercion, retaliation, humiliation, staffing shortages or staff convenience. In California, a 2024 law (S.B. 1043) requires short-term residential therapeutic programs to report each use of manual restraint to the state licensing agency.

### Medical concerns

- Your child reports pain, weight loss, exhaustion or illness that does not seem to be treated.
- Medications are started, stopped or changed without clear communication with you.
- You have trouble getting basic medical information or the name of the treating provider.

### Sudden transports

- Your child is moved to another program, another state or a "transition" placement with little notice.
- You learn your child was transported by a third-party company at night or without warning.

## Practical steps you can take now

### Request records in writing

Send a short, polite written request (email is fine) for your child's records, including:

- Medical and medication records
- Therapy and treatment plans
- Incident reports, including any restraint or seclusion
- Communication logs and any notes about your child's calls or letters

Health care providers covered by HIPAA must generally give patients access to their health records, and a parent is generally treated as a minor child's personal representative, with exceptions. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services says a provider normally has up to 30 days to respond. Not every program is a HIPAA-covered provider, so put your request in writing either way and save the program's reply.

### Ask about licensing

Ask the program, in writing, which state agency licenses it and what its license type and number are. Ask the same of any transport company that moved your child.

### Check state licensing databases yourself

You do not have to take the program's word for it.

- **Utah:** The Division of Licensing and Background Checks, part of the Utah Department of Health and Human Services, has a public "Find a Facility" search. According to the division, the search shows the prior 36 months of licensing compliance history, and older records can be requested. The division also licenses youth transportation companies.
- **California:** The California Department of Social Services, Community Care Licensing Division, has a public facility search. The department says complaint investigation reports on facilities are available through the site, and that some details, such as citations, may require calling a regional office.

If your child is in another state, search that state's human services or child care licensing agency for a similar tool.

### Know your right to visit and to remove your child

If you placed your child privately, you are generally still the parent with legal authority over your child. Ask the program for its visiting policy in writing, and read the enrollment contract for what it says about visits, withdrawal, notice and fees. If you believe your child is unsafe, safety comes before contract terms; you can arrange to bring your child home and sort out the rest afterward. If a court or a public agency ordered the placement, different rules apply, and you should talk with your child's caseworker or a lawyer before acting.

### Keep a log

Start a simple dated log. Write down:

- Each call or visit, who was present, and what your child said
- Every letter received or sent
- Any injuries, illnesses or medication changes you learn about
- Who you spoke with at the program, and what they told you
- Copies or screenshots of emails, texts and portal messages

Keep originals unedited. A clear log helps you, your child's doctors and any lawyer you later consult.

## When to call a lawyer

Consider a confidential consultation if:

- Your child reports abuse, restraint, seclusion or neglect.
- Your child was injured or became seriously ill.
- The program refuses to share records or licensing information.
- Your child was moved without your informed agreement.
- You were told things about the program that turned out not to be true.

A lawyer can help with records requests, licensing complaints, and whether any claims may exist. Deadlines vary by state, claim and defendant, so it helps to ask early. A consultation does not commit you to anything.

**Please don't put sensitive details in web forms or emails.** A short message asking for a call back is enough. Details can be discussed by phone in a confidential conversation.

## FAQ

### Is it normal for a program to monitor my calls?

Some supervision may be part of a program's design, but constant monitoring, cut-off calls, or being told not to believe your child are reasons to ask more questions. Some states, including Utah, require licensed programs to facilitate confidential communication between a child and parents.

### The program says my child is "manipulating" me. What should I do?

Listen to your child, write down what you hear, and look for facts you can check, such as records, licensing history and injuries. You can take your child seriously and still verify what you are told.

### Can I bring my child home before the program says they're ready?

In a private placement, parents generally keep legal authority over their child, though the contract may address notice or fees. If the placement was ordered by a court or agency, talk with the caseworker or a lawyer first. If your child is in immediate danger, call 911 or local police.
