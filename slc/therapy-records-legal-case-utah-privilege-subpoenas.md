Sooner or later, a client's legal case may reach your office. It might be a letter from a lawyer asking for records, a subpoena with a short deadline, or a client asking whether what they say in session could end up in court.

This article explains the basic Utah framework so you can respond calmly and protect both your client and yourself. It is written for LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists.

If you are new to this series, you may want to start with [A Therapist's Referral Guide: When a Utah Client's Trauma May Involve a Legal Claim](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

## The privilege belongs to your client

Utah Rule of Evidence 506 creates a physician and mental health therapist–patient privilege. Under the rule, a patient may refuse to disclose, and may prevent others from disclosing, information communicated in confidence for diagnosis or treatment. That covers diagnoses, treatment and advice, as well as information shared among the patient, the provider and others taking part in care under the provider's direction, such as family members.

The rule's definition of "mental health therapist" includes licensed psychologists, clinical and certified social workers, marriage and family therapists, professional counselors and certain advanced practice psychiatric mental health nurses who diagnose or treat a mental or emotional condition.

Two points matter most in practice:

- **The patient holds the privilege.** The patient, or a guardian or conservator, may claim it.
- **You may assert it for the patient.** During the patient's life, the rule presumes the therapist has authority to claim the privilege on the patient's behalf. When a request arrives without your client's consent, raising the privilege is often the right first step while you get advice.

Utah's licensing statute points the same way. Utah Code 58-60-114 generally bars a mental health therapist from disclosing confidential communications without express written consent, with listed exceptions, including disclosures required or permitted by law and disclosures in legal proceedings that fall within an exception to Rule 506.

## When the privilege does not apply

Rule 506 lists exceptions. The one clinicians see most in civil cases is the "condition as an element" exception.

### When the client's condition is part of the claim

Under Rule 506(d)(1), there is no privilege for a communication when a party shows, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the communication is relevant to the patient's physical, mental or emotional condition, and that condition is an element of a claim or defense. After a patient's death, the exception can apply when a party relies on the condition as an element of a claim or defense.

In plain terms: if your client files a lawsuit claiming emotional harm, the other side may argue that some of your records are now relevant. That does not mean everything in the chart becomes public. It means a court may be asked to decide what, if anything, must be produced, and under what protections.

### Other exceptions

Rule 506 also has exceptions for certain criminal matters, for proceedings to hospitalize a patient, and for communications made during a court-ordered examination.

A client thinking about a legal claim should understand, before filing, that their mental health history may become part of the case. That is one reason an early, confidential conversation with a lawyer helps.

## HIPAA: psychotherapy notes are different

HIPAA draws a line between the general medical record and "psychotherapy notes."

Under 45 CFR 164.501, psychotherapy notes are notes recorded by a mental health professional documenting or analyzing the contents of a counseling session, kept separate from the rest of the individual's medical record. The definition excludes:

- Medication prescription and monitoring
- Session start and stop times
- Modalities and frequency of treatment
- Results of clinical tests
- Summaries of diagnosis, functional status, treatment plan, symptoms, prognosis and progress to date

Under 45 CFR 164.508(a)(2), most uses and disclosures of psychotherapy notes require the patient's specific authorization, with limited exceptions. HHS has explained that one exception covers disclosures required by other law, such as mandatory abuse reporting.

Two cautions. First, notes only qualify if they are actually kept separate. Process notes stored in the main chart are generally treated as part of the record. Second, HIPAA is a floor. Rule 506 and Utah law also apply, and the more protective rule often governs.

## Subpoenas vs. court orders

A subpoena and a court order are not the same thing, and the difference matters.

### A subpoena

A subpoena is often issued by a lawyer, not signed by a judge. Under HIPAA's rule for judicial proceedings (45 CFR 164.512(e)), a covered entity may respond to a subpoena that is not accompanied by a court order only if it receives certain "satisfactory assurances," such as proof that the patient was given notice and a chance to object, or that a qualified protective order has been sought. HHS has published guidance on what those assurances look like.

Even when HIPAA's conditions are met, Utah's privilege rules and licensing statute still apply. A subpoena compels a response. It does not automatically mean you must hand over privileged records. A response can be an objection or an assertion of privilege.

### A court order

A court order is signed by a judge and generally carries more weight. Read it carefully: orders can be limited in scope, may send records to the court rather than a party, or may include protective terms.

### What not to do

- Do not ignore a subpoena or order. Deadlines are often short.
- Do not call the requesting lawyer and discuss your client.
- Do not send the whole chart "to be safe."

## When a lawyer asks for records

Requests often come from the client's own lawyer. Even then, follow a process.

1. **Look for a valid authorization.** For anything beyond basic information, you want a signed authorization from your client that names what may be released, to whom, and for what purpose. Psychotherapy notes need their own specific authorization.
2. **Talk with your client.** Confirm they know about the request and understand what will be shared. Many clients do not realize what is in their records.
3. **Release only what is authorized.** Send what the authorization covers, no more.
4. **Call for help early.** Your malpractice carrier may offer a risk-management line, and your own counsel can review a subpoena or order before you respond. Your licensing board and professional association may also have guidance.
5. **Keep a record.** Note what you received, what you released, when and to whom.

## Document clinically, not forensically

Your notes exist to support treatment. Write them that way.

- Record what the client reported and what you observed, using the client's words where useful.
- Avoid conclusions about who did what, or whether an institution is liable. That is not your role, and it can read as advocacy.
- Keep notes timely, accurate and professional. Assume a judge may read them someday.
- Do not change past entries. If you need to correct something, use a dated addendum.

## Treating therapist vs. retained evaluator

Lawyers sometimes ask a treating therapist to "just give an opinion" about causation or future harm. Be careful.

A treating clinician's role is to help the client. A retained evaluator or forensic evaluator is hired to assess a question for a legal case, often with a duty to the court and to objectivity. These roles differ in purpose, relationship and method.

The American Psychological Association's forensic psychology guidelines address this directly. Guideline 4.02.01, on therapeutic-forensic role conflicts, notes that providing both services to the same person can impair objectivity or cause harm. It encourages psychologists to disclose that risk and, where feasible, refer the forensic request to another qualified provider. Other disciplines have similar ethical concerns about dual roles.

You may still be asked to testify as a fact witness about the treatment you provided, which is different from giving forensic opinions. If you are unsure which role is being requested, ask for the request in writing and get your own advice before agreeing.

## FAQ

### Can I refuse to turn over records in response to a subpoena?

You can object or assert the privilege on your client's behalf, and in many cases that is appropriate. What you should not do is ignore it. Get advice from your own counsel or your malpractice carrier before the deadline.

### Are my psychotherapy notes automatically protected?

They have extra protection under HIPAA if they meet the definition, including being kept separate from the main record. They are not immune from every legal process. A court may still address them under Utah's privilege rules.

### My client's own lawyer wants the whole chart. Should I send it?

Only what your client has authorized in writing. Talk with your client first. Many clients and lawyers find that a treatment summary or specific records meet the need.

### If my client sues, does the privilege disappear?

Not entirely. Under Rule 506(d)(1), communications relevant to a condition that is an element of a claim or defense may lose protection, but a party must make that showing, and courts can limit and protect what is produced.

### Can I serve as my client's forensic evaluator?

Generally, this raises serious dual-role concerns. Professional guidance encourages referring forensic work to a separate, qualified evaluator where feasible. Talk with your own counsel or ethics consultant before agreeing.
