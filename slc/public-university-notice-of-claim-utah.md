When a client's harm involves a university, one question shapes almost everything that follows: is the school public or private?

For a private school, the usual rules for civil claims generally apply. For a public school, Utah's Governmental Immunity Act adds a separate layer. Before a client can file most state-law claims, they must first file a formal notice of claim, with the right recipient, in the right form, within a set time. Miss that step, and a claim can be lost before it ever reaches a courtroom.

This article explains the basics for Utah LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists. The goal is not to make you a legal resource. It is to explain why, when a public school is involved, an early referral matters most.

## Which Utah schools are public?

Utah's public colleges and universities are part of the Utah System of Higher Education. They include:

- University of Utah
- Utah State University, including USU Eastern and its other statewide campuses
- Weber State University
- Southern Utah University
- Utah Valley University
- Utah Tech University
- Snow College
- Salt Lake Community College

The system also includes Utah's public technical colleges.

Many well-known Utah schools are private, including:

- Brigham Young University
- Westminster University (formerly Westminster College)
- Ensign College
- Western Governors University

The same "public entity" question can come up with public K–12 schools, school districts, and state- or county-run programs. If you are not sure whether an institution is public, that is a good question for a lawyer to answer early.

## The Utah Governmental Immunity Act in brief

The Governmental Immunity Act of Utah is found in Title 63G, Chapter 7 of the Utah Code. In general terms, it starts from the idea that governmental entities are immune from suit, then lists specific situations where that immunity is waived.

Utah Code 63G-7-301 lists those waivers. Among others, it includes certain injuries caused by a governmental employee's negligent act or omission within the scope of employment, and certain injuries caused by dangerous or defective conditions of public buildings and other public improvements. Each waiver comes with conditions and exceptions, and other parts of the Act keep immunity in place for some kinds of claims.

Whether a waiver applies to a particular situation is a fact-specific legal question. For clinicians, the takeaway is simpler: claims against public entities follow their own rulebook.

## The notice-of-claim requirement

### What the statute requires

Under Utah Code 63G-7-401, a person with a claim against a governmental entity, or against its employee for acts within the scope of employment, must file a written notice of claim before bringing suit.

The notice must include a brief statement of the facts, the nature of the claim, and the damages incurred so far as they are known. If a claim is being pursued against an employee individually, the notice must name that employee. It must be signed by the claimant or by an authorized person, such as the claimant's attorney, parent or legal guardian.

### Where the notice goes

Section 63G-7-401 also says who must receive the notice. For example, a claim against the state goes to the attorney general. A claim against another public board, commission or body goes to a member of its governing board, its executive director, or its executive secretary. Entities can also designate an agent to receive notices.

Governmental entities are required to file a statement with the Division of Corporations and Commercial Code listing the office or agent that receives notices, a physical address, and an email address. The statute allows delivery by hand, by mail, or by email to the addresses on file, and when the notice is emailed, it requires a copy to be sent to the entity's attorney at the same time.

Which recipient is correct for a particular public university, and how to confirm the current listing, is something a lawyer should handle. Small errors in the recipient, delivery method or contents can create serious problems.

### The time limit

Utah Code 63G-7-402 states that a claim against a governmental entity, or against its employee for acts within the scope of employment, is barred unless a notice of claim is filed under Section 63G-7-401 **within one year after the claim arises**.

When a claim "arises" is defined in Section 63G-7-401. In general, it is when the statute of limitations that would apply to a private person begins to run. The statute also provides that this period does not begin until the claimant knew, or with reasonable diligence should have known, that they had a claim and who the governmental entity or employee was. The claimant bears the burden of showing reasonable diligence.

Applying that rule to real facts is not simple. Clients should not try to calculate the date on their own. And notice is only the first step: the Act has additional requirements and deadlines that apply after a notice is filed.

## Federal claims exist separately

Some claims involving public universities may arise under federal law instead of, or in addition to, state law. Two examples:

- **Title IX** prohibits sex discrimination in education programs that receive federal funding. It applies to both public and many private schools, and it can be relevant to how a school responds to sexual harassment or assault and to retaliation after a report.
- **Section 1983** (42 U.S.C. 1983) allows claims against people who, acting under color of state law, violate federal constitutional or statutory rights. Its application to public universities and their employees has important limits.

Federal claims follow their own rules and deadlines, which differ from the state notice-of-claim process. A lawyer needs to evaluate them separately. The existence of a federal claim does not make the state deadline go away, and vice versa.

## Why early referral matters most with a public school

With most institutional claims, sooner is better. With a public university, it can be decisive.

- **The notice clock may already be running.** A client who waits to "feel ready" may lose the ability to bring a state-law claim.
- **Survivors often delay for good reasons.** Shame, fear of school consequences, academic pressure, and the hope that the school will fix it can all push a client to wait. Those reasons are understandable. They do not pause the statute.
- **The school's own process is not a substitute.** Reporting to a Title IX office or campus police is not the same as filing a notice of claim under the Governmental Immunity Act.
- **Records and memories fade.** Early contact helps a lawyer request and preserve information.

A consult does not commit the client to anything. It gives them information so they can decide on their own timeline, with the deadlines in view.

## FAQ

### How long does a client have to file a notice of claim against a Utah public university?

Utah Code 63G-7-402 requires the notice to be filed within one year after the claim arises. When a claim "arises" depends on the facts and on Section 63G-7-401, so a client should talk with a lawyer rather than calculate it on their own.

### Is BYU a public university?

No. Brigham Young University is a private university. Utah's public universities include the University of Utah, Utah State University, Weber State, Southern Utah University, Utah Valley University, Utah Tech, Snow College and Salt Lake Community College.

### Does reporting to the school's Title IX office count as a notice of claim?

No. A Title IX report and a notice of claim under the Governmental Immunity Act are different things with different purposes. One does not replace the other.

### Do federal claims like Title IX have the same deadline?

Federal claims have their own rules and limitation periods, which differ from the state notice-of-claim process. A lawyer should review them separately.

### Can a therapist file the notice for a client?

The statute requires the notice to be signed by the claimant or certain authorized people, such as an attorney, parent or legal guardian. Clinicians should not prepare or file legal notices for clients. The safer step is a prompt referral.
