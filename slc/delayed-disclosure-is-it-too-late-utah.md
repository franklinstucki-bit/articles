"It was so long ago. Is it too late to do anything?"

Clinicians hear this question in many forms. Sometimes it comes in the first session. More often it comes after months of work, when a client finally names what happened and starts to wonder what it means.

This article is for Utah LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists. It summarizes what research says about delayed disclosure of sexual abuse, explains in general terms how legal timing works, and offers language for responding without pressure. It is part of a series that begins with [A Therapist's Referral Guide](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

## Delayed disclosure is common

Waiting years, or decades, to tell anyone about sexual abuse is not unusual. It is one of the most consistent findings in the research.

- **The CDC** notes that many children wait to report or never report child sexual abuse, which means official numbers likely understate the problem. The CDC also reports that about 90% of child sexual abuse involves someone the child or family knows and trusts.
- **A widely cited review** by London, Bruck, Ceci and Shuman, published in *Psychology, Public Policy, and Law* in 2005, found that roughly 60% to 70% of adults with a history of childhood sexual abuse did not recall disclosing it as children. Only a small share recalled the abuse being reported to authorities.
- **Australia's Royal Commission** into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse reported that about one in 10 survivors who attended private sessions disclosed for the first time to the Commission itself. An early analysis of that data, presented in 2017, suggested survivors took about 22 years on average to disclose, with men taking longer than women.

For clinicians, the takeaway is simple: a late disclosure is not a sign that the account is less credible. It is the typical pattern.

## Why survivors wait

Research on disclosure, including a 2019 review by Alaggia, Collin-Vézina and Lateef covering studies from 2000 to 2016, describes disclosure as a process rather than a single event. Common barriers include:

- **Relationship to the abuser.** When the person who caused harm is a family member, leader or trusted adult, telling can feel like betrayal or put the survivor's home or community at risk.
- **Shame and self-blame.** Survivors may believe they invited or allowed the abuse.
- **Fear of not being believed.** This fear is often based on experience, especially if an earlier attempt to tell went badly.
- **Gender and cultural norms.** Boys and men may face additional stigma. Religious or community expectations can also discourage disclosure.
- **Lack of language.** Young children may not have the words. Some survivors only recognize the experience as abuse years later.
- **Institutional pressure.** When abuse happened in a school, church, program or team, the survivor may have been told to keep it internal.

Disclosure often happens in stages. A client may test the waters with a partial account, watch your response and share more later.

## Supporting the client in the room

When a client asks whether it is too late, the question is usually about more than law. It can mean, "Does what happened to me still matter?"

Some responses that help:

- **Validate the timing.** "Many people take years to talk about this. That's common, and it doesn't make your experience any less real."
- **Separate healing from legal decisions.** "Whatever you decide about legal options, our work here continues."
- **Avoid guessing about the law.** "I don't know the answer to the legal question, and the rules are complicated. A lawyer could tell you what applies to your situation."
- **Keep control with the client.** "You don't have to decide anything today."

Avoid telling a client that it is definitely too late, or definitely not too late. Neither statement is safe without a legal review.

## The legal side: how timing works in general

Every legal claim has a time limit, often called a statute of limitations. For sexual-abuse claims, those limits can be very different from other kinds of cases, and they have changed over time.

### Concepts that often matter

Without addressing any specific deadline, here are concepts lawyers commonly consider:

- **Age at the time of abuse.** Many states treat abuse of a minor differently from abuse of an adult. Some time periods do not begin until the survivor turns 18.
- **Discovery.** Some laws measure time from when a person discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, the injury and its connection to the abuse. Survivors sometimes do not connect present-day symptoms to childhood abuse until much later.
- **Who the defendant is.** Claims against an individual may follow different rules from claims against an institution that failed to protect, supervise or report.
- **Government defendants.** Claims against public entities, such as public schools or state-run programs, often require a formal notice of claim on a short timeline before a lawsuit can be filed.
- **Changes in the law.** Legislatures revise these rules. Whether a change applies to older abuse can depend on the exact wording of the new law and on court decisions.
- **Which state's law applies.** Abuse that happened in another state, or a claim against an organization based elsewhere, can raise questions about which state's rules control.

### Utah's recent changes

Utah's Legislature has revisited its civil time limits for sexual abuse more than once. In the 2025 General Session, lawmakers considered H.B. 117, Sexual Abuse Statutes of Limitation Amendments, which addressed civil statutes of limitation for certain sexual crimes. As introduced, it proposed a new section, Utah Code 78B-2-402, in place of an earlier provision.

We have not independently confirmed the bill's final enacted text, so this article does not state specific Utah time periods. The point for clinicians is that the rules have been moving, and older assumptions about what is "too late" may not hold. Only a lawyer reviewing the specific facts and the current statute can evaluate timing.

### Why earlier is better

Even when the law allows more time, waiting can make a case harder. Witnesses move or pass away. Records are destroyed under retention schedules. Organizations change hands. A consult does not commit a client to anything, but it can help preserve options.

## When to mention a legal consult

You do not need to decide whether a client has a claim. A consult may be worth mentioning when:

- The client asks directly whether anything can be done
- The abuse involved a school, church, youth program, employer or other organization
- The client says someone in authority knew and did not act
- The client is weighing a police report, a civil claim or both and wants information

Offer the option once, clearly and gently, and let the client lead from there.

## FAQ

### Is a delayed disclosure less credible?

Research consistently shows that delayed disclosure is common, and many survivors never disclose in childhood. A delay, on its own, says little about whether an account is true.

### Can I tell my client whether their claim is time-barred?

No. Timing depends on the type of claim, the survivor's age at the time, discovery, the defendant and changes in the law. Only a lawyer reviewing the facts can evaluate it.

### Did Utah change its sexual-abuse time limits?

Utah's Legislature has revisited these laws in recent years, including H.B. 117 in 2025. Because the rules have shifted, a client should not rely on older information about deadlines.

### What if my client isn't sure they want to take legal action?

That is fine. A consult is a confidential conversation to understand options. Many people talk with a lawyer and decide not to proceed, or decide to wait.

### Will a legal consult interfere with treatment?

It should not. Many clients find that accurate information eases anxiety. Clients can set the pace, and therapy continues alongside any legal process.
