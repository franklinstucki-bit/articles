Maybe you were sent to a wilderness program, a residential treatment center or a therapeutic boarding school in Utah as a teenager. Maybe you are the parent who signed the paperwork because you were told it was the safest choice. Years later, you may still be sorting out what happened there.

You are not alone, and it is not too late to ask questions.

**The short answer:** Adults who were harmed as teens in a Utah youth program, and the parents who placed them there, may have civil claims under Utah law. Whether a claim can still be brought depends on what happened, who was responsible, and which time limit applies. Utah has no time limit for suing the person who sexually abused a child, but claims against programs and other organizations usually have firm deadlines.

## Who Can Bring a Troubled-Teen Program Claim in Utah

### Former residents who are now adults

Most people who reach out are adults now. Some left a program last year. Others left decades ago. Being an adult today does not erase what happened when you were a minor, and Utah law has specific rules for injuries that happened to children.

You also do not need to live in Utah now (more on that below).

### Parents

Parents can have their own claims too. Families were often told one thing about a program's care, staffing or methods, and something very different was happening behind closed doors. A parent's claim may involve what they were promised, what they paid, and what they were not told. Many parents carry real guilt about the placement. Wanting answers now is not a betrayal of your child. It can be part of making things right.

## What Kinds of Harm Are Involved

Every case is different, but survivors of Utah youth programs commonly describe:

- Physical abuse, including improper restraints or force used as punishment
- Sexual abuse or exploitation by staff or by other residents the program failed to supervise
- Isolation or seclusion used to control or punish
- Denial of food, sleep, medical care or contact with family
- Emotional and psychological abuse, including humiliation and intimidation
- Injuries from unsafe conditions, especially in outdoor or wilderness settings

The legal question is usually not only "who hurt me?" but also "who let it happen?" That can include the program, its owners and managers, and others who were responsible for keeping you safe.

## Utah's Oversight Laws: S.B. 127 (2021) and Later Reforms

For years, Utah's youth treatment industry was criticized for weak oversight. In 2021, the Utah Legislature passed S.B. 127, Human Services Program Amendments. Among other changes, it:

- Required the state to inspect each congregate care program at least once per calendar quarter, with at least two of those inspections unannounced
- Required programs to report any use of restraint or seclusion, and any critical incident, to the state within one business day
- Required written policies on when and how restraint or seclusion may be used, with documentation and review
- Prohibited cruel, severe, unusual or unnecessary practices against children, including inducing pain, peer restraints, and denying meals, shelter or family contact as punishment
- Required programs to publicly post how to file a complaint with the state Office of Licensing

These rules now appear in Utah Code Title 26B, Chapter 2, including the inspection requirement in Section 26B-2-107 and the congregate care program rules in Section 26B-2-123. Utah defines a "congregate care program" to include outdoor youth programs, residential support and treatment programs, and therapeutic schools that serve children (Utah Code 26B-2-101).

In 2025, the Legislature followed up with S.B. 297, Congregate Care Amendments. It created a congregate care ombudsman to receive and look into concerns about programs, required programs to notify parents of a crisis, keep lists of authorized contacts and post the ombudsman's contact information, and protected people who report concerns.

When a program breaks rules written to protect children, that can be important evidence in a civil case. The rules that applied to you depend on when you were there.

## Utah's Time Limits for Childhood Sexual Abuse Claims

Utah Code 78B-2-308 sets the civil time limits for sexual abuse of a child:

- **Against the person who committed the abuse:** a survivor may file a civil lawsuit at any time.
- **Against others, such as a program or organization that negligently allowed the abuse:** the claim generally must be filed within four years after the survivor turns 18, or within four years after the survivor discovers the abuse if that happens later.

The statute also says that a parent's or guardian's knowledge is not held against a child under 18 when it comes to these deadlines.

The Legislature once tried to revive older claims against perpetrators that had already expired. The Utah Supreme Court held that the revival portion of the law was unconstitutional as applied to perpetrators. That ruling does not change the "any time" rule for claims that had not expired.

## The General Four-Year Deadline in Utah

Not every harm is sexual abuse. For claims that do not have a more specific deadline, Utah's catch-all time limit in Utah Code 78B-2-307 is four years. Different rules can apply depending on the type of claim, and when the clock starts can be a serious legal question.

If a program was run by a government entity, a written notice of claim may be required within one year after the claim arises (Utah Code 63G-7-402). That deadline is much shorter, so do not wait to ask.

Before you assume your time has run out, or that it hasn't, talk with a lawyer.

## How to Gather Records From a Utah Youth Program

You do not need to have everything before you call. But records help, and some take time to get. Here is where to start:

- **Your own memories, written down.** Names, dates, buildings, staff nicknames, other residents. Write what you remember without pressure to be perfect.
- **Family records.** Parents often kept enrollment contracts, brochures, emails, invoices, letters from the program and letters you wrote home.
- **Payment records.** Bank statements, loan papers, and insurance explanation-of-benefits forms can show dates and what was billed.
- **Medical and school records.** Records from doctors, hospitals and schools before, during and after the program can help show what changed.
- **Transport records.** If you were taken to the program by an escort or transport company, any paperwork about that.
- **State records.** Utah's Office of Licensing handles concerns about licensed programs and accepts public records requests under Utah's records law (GRAMA). A lawyer can help request licensing, inspection and child-protective records.
- **Photos and messages.** Anything from the time, including social media groups where former residents reconnect.

Keep originals safe and make copies. Do not post details of your case online while you are deciding what to do.

## FAQ

### Can I sue a troubled-teen program in Utah if I was there years ago?

Possibly. Under Utah Code 78B-2-308, a survivor can sue the person who sexually abused them as a child at any time. Claims against a program or organization generally must be filed within four years after turning 18, or within four years after discovering the abuse if later. Other kinds of claims have their own deadlines, so it is worth asking a Utah lawyer about your specific dates.

### Can parents sue a residential treatment center in Utah?

Parents may have their own claims, separate from their child's. These can involve what the program promised about care and safety, what the family paid, and what the program did not disclose. Whether a parent has a claim depends on the facts and the timing.

### What changed with Utah's troubled-teen laws in 2021?

Utah's 2021 S.B. 127 required at least quarterly state inspections of congregate care programs, with at least two unannounced. It also required programs to report restraint, seclusion and critical incidents within one business day, and it banned cruel or unnecessary practices against children. A 2025 law, S.B. 297, added a congregate care ombudsman and new family-notification rules.

### Do I have to live in Utah to bring a claim about a Utah youth program?

No. If the harm happened at a Utah program, Utah law may apply wherever you live today. Many former residents were sent to Utah from other states and handle their cases from home by phone and video.

### What records do I need for a troubled-teen program case?

You can start with what you have: your own written memories, family emails and contracts, payment and insurance records, and medical or school records. A lawyer can help request licensing and other state records. You do not need everything before you ask for help.

### Is there someone in Utah I can report a youth program to right now?

Yes. Utah's Department of Health and Human Services runs a Congregate Care Ombuds office for concerns about youth programs, and the Office of Licensing accepts concerns about licensed programs. If a child is in danger now, call 911 or Utah's child abuse hotline at 1-855-323-3237.
