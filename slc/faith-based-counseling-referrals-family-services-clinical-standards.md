Many Utah clients arrive in your office after some time in faith-based counseling. For members of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, that is often Family Services, the Church's counseling and welfare resource.

Understanding how a client's earlier care was structured can help you start well. Who referred them? Who knew they were in counseling? What did they agree to share, and with whom?

This article is for Utah mental health professionals, including LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists. It describes Family Services' model in its own published words, sets it next to the confidentiality and autonomy standards in the APA and ACA ethics codes, and offers neutral intake questions. It is not a critique of any faith or its members. Different models serve different purposes, and many clients value both.

It builds on the first article in this series, [A Therapist's Referral Guide: When a Utah Client's Trauma May Involve a Legal Claim](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

## A note on the name

The service was formerly called LDS Family Services. The Church's newsroom, in its guidance on aligning names with the full name of the Church, states that LDS Family Services "has been renamed Family Services." Clients may use either name.

## Family Services' model, in its own words

The descriptions below come from Family Services' own published page on the Church's website.

### Referral through a bishop

Family Services describes itself as "a welfare resource of the Church," and says that "as with other Church welfare services, the bishop has stewardship." It continues: "Therefore, a referral from a bishop is required." The page also refers to receiving "a referral from your bishop or stake president."

### Short-term counseling

The page states: "Counseling is short-term, typically up to 12 sessions." For longer-term or other needs, Family Services says it helps people find services in the community.

### Mission

Family Services describes its purpose this way: it "assists leaders to help members in developing emotional self-reliance and overcoming obstacles to making and keeping sacred covenants."

### Confidentiality and leader involvement

The page states that "Family Services maintains confidentiality and will not discuss a client's confidential information with anyone outside Family Services unless authorized by the client."

It also explains that "we prefer to work in collaboration with members and their leaders," and that Family Services "does encourage members to give formal permission for counselors to consult with their Church leaders." It adds: "It is up to the client whether this permission is granted."

## Secular clinical standards for comparison

Licensed clinicians in Utah work within professional ethics codes. Two of the most widely referenced are the American Psychological Association's Ethics Code and the American Counseling Association's Code of Ethics. A few provisions are especially relevant here.

### APA Principle E: respect for rights and dignity

Principle E states that psychologists "respect the dignity and worth of all people, and the rights of individuals to privacy, confidentiality, and self-determination."

### APA Standard 4.02: discussing the limits of confidentiality

Standard 4.02 requires psychologists to discuss the relevant limits of confidentiality and the foreseeable uses of the information generated in their work. It says that, unless not feasible or contraindicated, "the discussion of confidentiality occurs at the outset of the relationship."

### ACA A.2.a: informed consent

ACA Standard A.2.a states that "clients have the freedom to choose whether to enter into or remain in a counseling relationship and need adequate information about the counseling process and the counselor."

### ACA A.4.b: personal values

ACA Standard A.4.b states that counselors "are aware of—and avoid imposing—their own values, attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors."

## Putting the two side by side

These are different frameworks built for different purposes. A neutral comparison:

- **Entry point.** Family Services' published model uses a bishop referral. In private secular practice, clients typically self-refer or are referred by a range of sources.
- **Length.** Family Services describes short-term counseling, typically up to 12 sessions, with help finding community services for other needs. Secular treatment length is usually set by clinical need, the client's goals and practical factors.
- **Stated purpose.** Family Services' mission statement includes spiritual aims alongside emotional self-reliance. Secular codes center the client's own goals and self-determination.
- **Leader involvement.** Family Services says it keeps information confidential unless the client authorizes disclosure, and encourages, but does not require, permission to consult with Church leaders. In secular practice, any contact with a third party is likewise governed by the client's consent, and clients may not have thought about how releases signed in one setting compare with another.

None of this means either model is right or wrong for a particular person. It means a client moving from one setting to another may carry assumptions about who knows what, and who sets the goals. Those assumptions are worth surfacing.

## Questions to ask a new client

These questions are neutral. They are meant to clarify the client's history and protect their choices, not to judge earlier care.

### About the referral

- "How did you first get connected with counseling? Who suggested it?"
- "What was the reason for the referral, as you understood it?"

### About information sharing

- "Did you sign anything allowing your counselor to talk with a bishop, stake president or other leader?"
- "Do you know what was shared, and with whom?"
- "Is that permission still in place? Would you like it to be?"

### About goals

- "Who set the goals for that counseling: you, your counselor, a leader, or a mix?"
- "Do those goals still fit what you want now?"

### About your work together

- "Would you like our work to stay separate from your church community?"
- "Is there anyone you want me to be able to talk with, and anyone you do not?"

Then explain your own confidentiality rules and limits clearly, in writing and out loud, at the start, as your ethics code requires.

## When legal questions may come up

Most clients who used faith-based counseling will never have a legal issue connected to it. But a consult may be worth mentioning if a client:

- believes information was shared with leaders, family, a school or an employer without their consent;
- signed releases they did not understand or feel free to refuse;
- was discouraged from reporting abuse to authorities; or
- disclosed abuse in any setting and is now wondering about options.

Deadlines vary by claim and defendant, and some are short. Utah's legislature has revisited sexual-abuse limitation laws in recent sessions, including H.B. 117 in 2025. An early consult protects options without committing the client to anything.

## FAQ

### Does Family Services require a bishop's referral?

Yes, according to its own published page, which says "a referral from a bishop is required" because the bishop has stewardship over Church welfare services.

### Does Family Services share counseling information with bishops?

Its published page says it will not discuss a client's confidential information outside Family Services unless the client authorizes it. It also says it encourages members to give formal permission for counselors to consult with Church leaders, and that granting permission is up to the client.

### How long is Family Services counseling?

Its page describes counseling as "short-term, typically up to 12 sessions," and says it helps people find community services for other needs.

### How should I talk about a client's previous faith-based counseling?

With curiosity and respect. Ask who referred them, what was shared and with whom, whether any releases are still in place, and whether earlier goals still fit. Avoid judging the earlier care or the client's faith.

### Can I coordinate with a client's religious leader?

Only with the client's informed, written consent, and only to the extent the client wants. Clarify the scope of any release and revisit it as treatment goes on.
