When a college student or former student tells you about a sexual assault, the conversation often turns to the school. Should they report? To whom? What happens next? And is a school's Title IX process the same thing as "doing something legally"?

It is not. This article explains the difference in plain terms for Utah mental health professionals, including LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists. It covers how Title IX processes work, how civil claims are different, why it matters whether the school is public or private, and how you can support a client through either path.

If you are new to this series, start with [A Therapist's Referral Guide: When a Utah Client's Trauma May Involve a Legal Claim](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

## Two different tracks

### Title IX: the school's administrative process

Title IX is a federal law that prohibits sex discrimination in education programs that receive federal funding. Schools covered by it must respond to sexual harassment, which includes sexual assault, and must have grievance procedures.

When a student reports to a Title IX office, the school runs its own administrative process. Depending on the school and the facts, that can include supportive measures (such as changes to class schedules or housing), an investigation, a hearing, and discipline for the person found responsible.

A few points clients often misunderstand:

- **It is the school's process, not a court.** The school decides whether its own policy was violated.
- **The remedies are limited to what the school can do.** Discipline, accommodations and campus measures. A school's Title IX process generally does not award money damages to the person who reported.
- **The rules change.** Federal Title IX regulations have been revised several times in recent years, and each school's procedures can change with them.
- **Separate federal complaints exist.** A student can also complain to federal agencies about how a school handled a report. Those processes have their own timelines.

### Civil claims: the court system

A civil claim is a lawsuit, usually seeking money damages, filed in state or federal court. It might be against the person who caused the harm, an institution whose failures contributed to it, or both. Some claims arise under Title IX itself. Others arise under state law, such as negligence.

Civil claims are separate from a school's process. A student can pursue one, both or neither. A school's finding, either way, does not by itself decide whether a civil claim exists. And a civil claim has its own deadlines, which do not wait for a school's process to finish.

### Criminal reports are a third track

Reporting to police is different from both. A criminal case is brought by the government, not the survivor. Clients sometimes assume they must choose one path. They usually do not.

## Public vs. private schools in Utah

Whether a school is public or private can change the rules for a civil claim.

### Public universities

Utah's public institutions include the University of Utah, Utah Valley University and Utah State University, among others. Under the Utah Governmental Immunity Act, the "state" includes its colleges and universities. That means claims against these schools are generally claims against a governmental entity.

Claims against governmental entities come with special requirements. Utah Code 63G-7-401 and 63G-7-402 require a written **notice of claim** to be filed before a lawsuit can be brought, and the notice must be filed within a short period after the claim arises. The notice must meet specific content and delivery requirements. If it is not done correctly and on time, the claim can be barred.

That is why timing matters so much when a public school is involved. A client who waits for a Title IX process to finish before speaking with a lawyer may lose options.

### Private universities

Private schools, such as Brigham Young University, are generally not governmental entities, so the governmental notice-of-claim rules usually do not apply to claims against the school itself. Other deadlines still do.

There is a wrinkle. A 2019 Utah law, S.B. 197, treats a certified police department of a private college or university as a law enforcement agency and brings law enforcement agencies within Utah's records law and Governmental Immunity Act. If a private campus police department was involved, a lawyer should look at the timeline early.

## Federal oversight of Utah schools

Federal agencies sometimes review how schools handle reports. In 2017, the U.S. Department of Justice opened a Title IX compliance review of Utah State University. The review followed allegations that the university failed to respond to numerous reports of serious student-on-student sexual assault. In February 2020, the Department announced it had concluded USU did not comply with Title IX. USU entered an agreement to revise policies, respond to known harassment, train students and staff, conduct climate surveys and support its Title IX coordinators. The agreement ran through the 2022–2023 academic year.

Reviews like this address a school's systems. They are not the same as an individual's civil claim, and they do not resolve one.

## Deadlines: say "soon," not "someday"

Utah's legislature has revisited sexual-abuse limitation laws in recent sessions, including H.B. 117 in 2025. Deadlines vary by the type of claim, the defendant, the survivor's age when the harm occurred and other facts. Some are short, especially when a public entity is involved.

The safest message for clients: if you are even wondering about legal options, talk to a lawyer early. A consult does not commit anyone to a lawsuit.

## What clinicians can document

Your records are clinical records first. They are not written for a lawsuit, and they should not be. Still, good clinical documentation can matter later if a client decides to pursue a claim.

Helpful habits:

- **Record what the client reports, in their words when practical.** Use quotation marks for direct statements.
- **Document symptoms, functioning and changes over time.** Sleep, concentration, attendance, relationships, work or school performance.
- **Note dates when known.** When symptoms began, when the client first disclosed, and when sessions occurred.
- **Separate observation from interpretation.** "Client tearful, avoided eye contact" is different from a conclusion about what happened.
- **Avoid legal conclusions.** Words like "negligent," "liable" or "the school failed" belong to lawyers and courts.
- **Keep records complete and unaltered.** Late entries should be dated as late entries.

Remember that records can be requested in litigation, and the client may later read them. Write as you always should: accurately, respectfully and with the client's dignity in mind.

## FAQ

### If my client files a Title IX report, does that count as a lawsuit?

No. A Title IX report starts the school's administrative process. A civil lawsuit is filed in court and is separate. Filing one does not file the other.

### Does a client need to finish the Title IX process before talking to a lawyer?

No. Waiting can be risky, because civil deadlines, including notice-of-claim deadlines for public schools, keep running during a school's process.

### Is the University of Utah treated differently from BYU?

Often, yes. Public universities like the University of Utah, Utah Valley University and Utah State University are governmental entities under Utah law, which brings notice-of-claim requirements. BYU is private, so different rules generally apply. A lawyer can explain which rules fit a specific situation.

### What if the school found the accused person "not responsible"?

A school's finding does not by itself decide whether a civil claim exists. Civil courts apply their own standards and rules. The client should get legal advice about their own facts.

### Will my therapy notes be used in a case?

They could be requested if a client brings a claim, though there are privacy protections and procedures. Talk with your own counsel or professional association about responding to records requests, and always involve the client.
