You may be the first professional a survivor trusts with the whole story. Sometimes that story includes more than the harm itself: a school that did not act, a program that hid what happened, or a report that seemed to make things worse. Those details can matter legally, and the client may not know it.

This guide is for Utah mental health professionals, including LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists. It is not about turning therapy into case-building. It is about knowing when a client might benefit from a confidential conversation with a lawyer, and how to offer that option without pressure.

## Why this matters for clinicians

When a university, youth program, church setting or school was involved, the client may be dealing with two injuries: the abuse or assault, and the way the institution responded. Survivors often carry questions they never say out loud. Will I get in trouble? Is it too late? A brief legal consult can answer some of those questions and may protect options that could be lost by waiting.

## Signs a client's trauma may involve legal claims

You do not need to decide whether a client has a case. That is the lawyer's job. But certain themes tend to come up in matters with legal significance.

### Institutional failure to protect

- The client says staff, faculty, coaches, leaders or administrators knew or suspected abuse and did nothing.
- The person who caused the harm had a history of complaints the institution was aware of.
- The harm happened in a setting the institution controlled, such as a dorm, residential program, school building, transport van or church activity.
- The client was a minor in a residential, wilderness or "troubled-teen" program and describes isolation, restraint, restricted communication with family, or punishment for reporting.

### Retaliation or consequences after reporting

- The client reported and then faced discipline, an investigation of their own conduct, loss of a scholarship, housing or job, or social or religious consequences.
- The client was told to stay quiet, sign something, or handle it "internally."
- The client describes a school, employer or program sharing their report with people they did not expect.

### Records and information issues

- The client was denied records, or records seem incomplete or changed.
- The client signed releases or statements under pressure, or does not remember what they signed.
- Information from a confidential setting seems to have reached a school, employer or ecclesiastical leader without the client's consent.

### Timing concerns

- The client mentions they "might do something about it someday."
- The harm involved a public entity, such as a public university or a government-run program.

None of these signs means a lawsuit is appropriate. They mean the client may have questions worth asking.

## Why Utah reporters may fear institutional consequences

Many Utah survivors attended, or still attend, schools with conduct codes tied to religious standards. Clinicians should understand why some clients hesitate to report, even years later.

In 2016, students at Brigham Young University said that reporting a sexual assault had led to Honor Code investigations of their own conduct. BYU formed an advisory council to study the issue. Its work found that the Title IX office had, in some cases, shared information with the Honor Code Office.

On October 26, 2016, BYU announced that it had accepted all 23 of the council's recommendations. Those included an amnesty clause stating that "being a victim of sexual misconduct is never a violation of the Honor Code," a new Title IX office located separately from the Honor Code Office, and limits on sharing a complainant's information with the Honor Code Office without consent.

Separately, a Utah Peace Officer Standards and Training (POST) investigation found that a former BYU police lieutenant had passed along information from Provo, Orem and Utah County police reports to BYU's Dean of Students, Title IX and Honor Code offices between August 2014 and June 2016. In February 2019, the Utah Department of Public Safety announced it would decertify BYU's police department. BYU appealed, and in January 2021 an administrative judge dismissed the decertification effort, allowing the department to continue operating.

BYU is not the only Utah school that has faced federal scrutiny. The U.S. Department of Justice opened a Title IX compliance review of Utah State University in 2017 based on allegations that it failed to respond to reports of student-on-student sexual assault. In February 2020, the Department announced it had concluded USU did not comply with Title IX, and USU entered a resolution agreement that ran through the 2022–2023 academic year.

**What this means in the therapy room:** Policies have changed, but fears have not always caught up. A client may still worry that reporting will lead to discipline, loss of standing, or exposure to family or church leaders. Name that fear gently, and let the client know a lawyer can explain how reporting and legal options work today.

## Faith-based counseling referrals: questions worth asking

Many Utah clients have received counseling through Family Services, which was formerly called LDS Family Services. In its own published description, Family Services says:

- A referral from a bishop is required.
- Counseling is short-term, "typically up to 12 sessions."
- Its mission includes helping members in "developing emotional self-reliance and overcoming obstacles to making and keeping sacred covenants."
- For other or ongoing counseling, it helps leaders and members find community services.

Secular clinical ethics codes frame the counseling relationship around the client. The American Counseling Association's Code of Ethics says clients "have the freedom to choose whether to enter into or remain in a counseling relationship" and that counselors avoid imposing their own values. The American Psychological Association's Ethics Code emphasizes respect for "privacy, confidentiality, and self-determination" and requires psychologists to discuss the limits of confidentiality at the outset.

These are different models with different purposes. When a client comes to you after faith-based counseling, a few neutral questions can help:

- Who made the original referral, and why?
- What did the client share with ecclesiastical leaders, and did they understand who would see it?
- Did the client sign a release, and do they know what information it covered?
- Does the client want their current treatment kept separate from religious leaders?
- Has anyone discouraged the client from reporting or from seeking outside help?

The answers can matter clinically. They can also matter legally if information moved without consent or if a report was discouraged.

## Utah reporting and confidentiality basics

Here is a short summary of a few Utah provisions that often come up in this work. It is not a complete statement of your obligations.

### Child abuse and neglect

Utah Code 80-2-602 requires a person who has reason to believe a child is, or has been, the subject of abuse or neglect to immediately report it to the Division of Child and Family Services or to the nearest peace officer or law enforcement agency. The statute includes a limited exception for clergy regarding certain confessions made directly by the perpetrator, and that exception does not cover information learned from other sources.

### Vulnerable adults

Utah Code 26B-6-205 requires a person who has reason to believe a vulnerable adult is, or has been, the subject of abuse, neglect or exploitation to immediately report it to Adult Protective Services or to the nearest peace officer or law enforcement agency.

### Duty to warn

Utah Code 78B-3-502 limits a therapist's duty to warn or protect to situations where a client communicates an actual threat of physical violence against a clearly identified or reasonably identifiable victim. The statute describes how that duty is discharged, including reasonable efforts to communicate the threat to the victim and notifying law enforcement.

### Your own obligations

Reporting rules have exceptions and nuances, and they change. Please confirm your obligations with your own counsel, your licensing board, or your professional association. A lawyer the client consults works for the client, not for you.

## Deadlines: why an early consult matters

Utah's legislature has taken up changes to sexual-abuse filing deadlines in several recent sessions, including H.B. 117 in 2025. These rules have shifted over time, and the deadline that applies depends on the type of claim, the age of the survivor when the harm occurred, when it was discovered, and who the defendant is.

Deadlines vary by claim and defendant, and some are short. Claims involving institutions can carry their own rules. Claims against government entities, which can include public universities and state-run programs, usually require a formal notice of claim before a lawsuit, on a timeline that is easy to miss.

The practical takeaway: if a client is even considering legal options, an early consult is better than a late one. A consult does not commit the client to anything.

## How to refer a client

A referral should always be the client's choice. Here is a simple approach:

1. **Ask permission to raise it.** "Some people in situations like yours find it helpful to talk with a lawyer, just to understand their options. Would it be okay if I shared some information about that?"
2. **Offer, don't push.** Make clear that therapy continues either way.
3. **Give the client the contact information** so they can reach out on their own:
   - Start at [slcsurvivorlaw.com](https://slcsurvivorlaw.com)
   - Or call (385) 585-5SLC, which is (385) 585-5752
4. **Suggest privacy.** Encourage clients not to put sensitive details in emails or web forms. A short message asking for a call back is enough. Details can be discussed by phone in a confidential consultation.
5. **Get written consent before sharing anything.** If the client wants you to talk with a lawyer or share records, obtain a signed release that specifies what may be shared.

**No referral fees are paid.** Frank Stucki, Esq., MBA does not pay or offer anything of value for referrals. You are simply giving your client an option.

In a consult, the client can share at their own pace, ask about deadlines and privacy, and decide whether to take any next step. Some clients schedule it so they can process it with you afterward.

## FAQ

### Do I need to know whether my client has a case before suggesting a consult?

No. You only need to notice that the client's experience may raise legal questions. Whether a claim exists is for the client and a lawyer to sort out.

### Will a legal consult interfere with treatment?

It should not. Many survivors find that getting accurate information reduces anxiety. Clients can pace the process, and therapy can continue alongside it.

### Can I talk to the lawyer about my client directly?

Only with your client's informed, written consent. Without a release, simply give the client the contact information and let them reach out.

### My client is afraid of school or church consequences. What can I tell them?

You can acknowledge the fear and let them know a lawyer can explain current policies, privacy protections and options. Some Utah schools have changed their reporting policies, but the client deserves a clear answer for their own situation.

### What if the abuse happened many years ago?

Utah's deadlines depend on the claim, the defendant and the facts, and the law has changed in recent years. The client should talk with a lawyer before assuming it is too late.
