Some clients mention it in passing. "I was sent to a program when I was fifteen." Others carry it for years before they say anything at all. For many adults who spent time in a residential treatment center, wilderness program or therapeutic boarding school, that experience sits underneath the presenting problem.

This article is for Utah LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists who are hearing these stories. It covers what former residents often describe, how Utah's oversight of these programs has changed, and when a client might benefit from a confidential conversation with a lawyer. It is the next piece in a series that began with [A Therapist's Referral Guide](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

## Why Utah clinicians hear these stories

Utah has long been a center of the youth residential treatment industry. In 2021, The Salt Lake Tribune reported that Utah had nearly 100 youth residential treatment centers and that more than 12,000 children had stayed in one over the prior five years, most of them from other states.

That means two groups may end up in your office: Utahns who were placed in local programs, and adults from elsewhere who were sent to Utah as teenagers and later stayed or returned.

## What the public record shows about the industry

Concerns about youth residential programs are not new, and they are not limited to Utah.

- In October 2007, the U.S. Government Accountability Office testified to Congress about thousands of allegations of abuse in certain residential programs for troubled youth, including wilderness programs, boot camps and academies, and examined cases in which teenagers died while enrolled.
- In June 2024, the U.S. Senate Finance Committee released a report titled "Warehouses of Neglect" after a two-year investigation of large youth residential treatment operators. It described physical and sexual abuse, improper restraint and seclusion, and oversight gaps at the state and federal levels.
- In February 2025, The Salt Lake Tribune and KSL reported that seven young people had died in Utah congregate care settings since the state's 2021 reform law, according to the bill sponsor and state health officials.

Not every program is the same, and many families and former residents describe programs as helpful. Your client's experience is their own. But the public record helps explain why some clients come to you guarded, and why their accounts deserve to be taken seriously.

## Utah's 2021 reform: S.B. 127

In 2021, the Utah Legislature passed S.B. 127, Human Services Program Amendments. The governor signed it on March 22, 2021, and it took effect May 5, 2021. It applies to "congregate care programs," which the bill defined to include outdoor youth programs, residential support programs, residential treatment programs and therapeutic schools that serve children.

As enrolled, S.B. 127:

- **Limited restraint and seclusion.** Programs must adopt written policies before using them. Chemical or mechanical restraint requires authorization from the state licensing office. Seclusion is limited to immediate safety needs when nothing less restrictive would work, and it may not be used for coercion, retaliation, humiliation, staffing shortages or staff convenience.
- **Banned certain practices.** The bill prohibited cruel, severe, unusual or unnecessary practices on a child, including inducing pain, peer restraints, and denying meals or shelter.
- **Required documentation and reporting.** Programs must document each use of restraint or seclusion and report it within one business day, along with critical incidents.
- **Increased inspections.** The licensing office must inspect each congregate care program at least once each calendar quarter, with at least two of those inspections unannounced.
- **Protected family contact.** Programs must facilitate weekly confidential communication between the child and family, consistent with the treatment plan, and may not use family contact as a reward or punishment.

These provisions were later moved into Title 26B, Chapter 2 of the Utah Code as part of the state's reorganization of health and human services laws.

## Later changes: S.B. 297 (2025)

In 2025, the Legislature passed S.B. 297, Congregate Care Amendments. As enrolled, it creates a congregate care ombudsman within the Department of Health and Human Services to receive and investigate reports about programs or staff. It requires programs to provide a telephone that a child, staff member or other person can use to call the ombudsman privately and without interference. It also requires programs to notify a parent or guardian no later than five hours after a child's crisis begins, creates an advisory committee and adds whistleblower protections.

## The state's licensing role

Utah's Department of Health and Human Services licenses these programs. The licensing function was long known as the Office of Licensing and now operates as the Division of Licensing and Background Checks (DLBC). DLBC accepts concerns about licensed programs through its website.

For clinicians, this matters in two ways. First, licensing and inspection records may exist for the period your client was in a program. Second, a current concern about a program can be reported to the state, separate from any legal claim.

## What former residents often describe

Adult survivors present in many ways. Some themes come up often.

### Delayed disclosure

Many clients do not describe their program experience until well into treatment. Some were told they were the problem. Some were not believed by parents who had paid for the placement. Others did not have words for what happened until years later.

### Institutional distrust

Researchers Carly Parnitzke Smith and Jennifer Freyd have used the term "institutional betrayal" to describe harm that occurs when an institution people depend on fails to prevent or respond to wrongdoing. Former residents may distrust therapists, treatment plans, level systems or anything that resembles the program. Intake paperwork and confidentiality forms can feel loaded.

### Common reports

Clients may describe:

- Restraint, isolation or "observation" placements
- Restricted or monitored contact with family
- Being transported to a program by strangers, sometimes at night
- Confrontational group sessions or public humiliation
- Punishment for reporting, or reports that went nowhere
- Sexual abuse by staff or peers

### Family complexity

Parents usually chose the placement, often on the advice of an educational consultant or after a crisis. Clients may hold grief, anger and loyalty toward the same people.

## Documenting the history

You are not an investigator, and you do not need to build a case. But careful clinical notes can be valuable to a client later.

- Record what the client tells you in their own words, with dates where they offer them.
- Note the approximate years, state and type of program, without pressing for details the client is not ready to share.
- Document symptoms and how they affect daily life.
- Avoid speculating about who is legally responsible.

If the client later chooses to pursue a claim, your records may be requested. Talk with your own counsel or licensing board about how to handle any request.

## When a legal consult may help

A legal consult may be worth mentioning when a client describes abuse, injury, unlawful restraint or seclusion, or a program that ignored reports. Depending on the facts, legal questions can involve more than the program itself, including:

- Individual staff members
- The company or companies that owned or operated the program
- Transport services that moved the youth to the program
- Consultants or placement services that recommended it

Whether any claim exists, and against whom, depends on the facts, the state where the program operated, and timing rules that vary by claim. Only a lawyer reviewing the specific situation can answer those questions. Deadlines can apply, so an earlier conversation keeps more options open.

## How to refer a client

A referral should always be the client's choice.

1. **Ask permission to raise it.** "Some people who went through programs like this talk with a lawyer to understand their options. Would it help if I shared that information?"
2. **Make clear therapy continues either way.**
3. **Share the contact information** so the client can reach out on their own: start at [slcsurvivorlaw.com](https://slcsurvivorlaw.com), or call (385) 585-5SLC, which is (385) 585-5752.
4. **Suggest privacy.** Encourage clients not to put sensitive details in emails or web forms. A short request for a call back is enough.
5. **Get written consent** before you share any information with a lawyer.

## FAQ

### My client was placed in a program outside Utah. Can they still talk to a Utah lawyer?

Yes. A consult can help sort out which state's law may apply and whether a lawyer licensed elsewhere is needed. Frank Stucki, Esq., MBA is licensed in California, Nevada, Washington and Utah.

### Does my client need records from the program before a consult?

No. Many former residents have no records at all. A lawyer can explain what records may exist and how they might be requested.

### Is the program's licensing history public?

Some licensing and inspection information may be available from the state. A lawyer can help identify what exists for a given time period.

### My client's parents chose the program. Does a consult mean suing family?

No. A consult is a confidential conversation about options. Clients decide what, if anything, to do next.

### What if the client was in a program many years ago?

Timing rules depend on the claim, the defendant and the facts, and the law has changed in recent years. The client should not assume it is too late without talking with a lawyer.
