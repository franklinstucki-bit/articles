Many Utah clients have a religious leader in their story. Sometimes that leader was a source of comfort. Sometimes the client told a bishop, pastor, priest or other leader about abuse and nothing seemed to happen. Clients may ask you why, or whether that leader was allowed to stay silent.

This article explains Utah's clergy exception to child-abuse reporting in plain terms, what it does and does not cover, and why it does not change your own duty as a clinician. It is written for Utah LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists, and it is part of a series that begins with [A Therapist's Referral Guide](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

This article is neutral toward every faith. Clergy of many traditions care deeply about protecting children. The goal here is accurate information, not criticism of any church.

## Utah's general duty to report

Utah Code 80-2-602 sets out the basic rule. If a person has reason to believe that a child is, or has been, the subject of abuse or neglect, the person must immediately report it to the Division of Child and Family Services or to the nearest peace officer or law enforcement agency.

The duty applies broadly. It is not limited to teachers, doctors or therapists.

## The clergy exception

The statute contains a narrow exception for clergy. Under Utah Code 80-2-602(3), the reporting requirement does not apply to a member of the clergy regarding a confession made to that clergy member while functioning in a ministerial capacity, without the consent of the person who confessed, if:

- The perpetrator made the confession directly to the member of the clergy, and
- The member of the clergy is, under canon law or church doctrine or practice, bound to maintain the confidentiality of that confession.

## The limits of the exception

The exception is narrower than many people assume. As published by the Utah Attorney General's Office, the statute provides:

- **Other sources must be reported.** If a member of the clergy receives information about abuse or neglect from any source other than the perpetrator's confession, the clergy member is required to report that information, even if the clergy member also heard about the abuse in a confession.
- **Ongoing abuse may be reported.** If a clergy member reasonably believes a child is the subject of ongoing abuse or neglect, the clergy member may report it, even if the information came from a confession.
- **Other legal duties remain.** The exception does not exempt the clergy member from other efforts required by law to prevent further abuse or neglect.
- **Privilege is a separate question.** The statute states that a clergy report is not intended to affect the application of privilege under the Utah Rules of Evidence.

### What this means for a survivor who told a religious leader

The exception, by its terms, is about confessions made by the perpetrator. When a survivor, a parent or another witness tells a religious leader about abuse, that is information from a source other than the perpetrator's confession. Under the statute, that information is subject to the reporting requirement.

That does not mean every leader who heard something did something wrong. Facts matter, including the survivor's age at the time, what was actually said, and when. But a survivor who disclosed to a religious leader should not assume the clergy exception closed every door.

## Recent legislative history

Utah lawmakers have taken up the clergy exception several times. Based on the Utah Legislature's bill records:

- **H.B. 90 (2020), Child Abuse Reporting Amendments.** This bill would have removed the clergy exemption. It did not pass; the House struck the enacting clause on March 12, 2020.
- **H.B. 115 (2023), Child Abuse Reporting Revisions.** This bill would also have removed the clergy exemption. It did not pass; the House struck the enacting clause on March 3, 2023.
- **H.B. 432 (2024), Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Amendments.** This bill kept the exception but added the provision allowing clergy to report when they reasonably believe abuse is ongoing, and addressed privilege. It passed both chambers without a dissenting vote and was signed by the governor on March 13, 2024.

So, as of this writing, the exception remains in Utah law in the narrowed form described above. Laws change, and readers should check the current statute.

## Your duty as a clinician is separate

The clergy exception applies only to members of the clergy acting in a ministerial capacity. It does not apply to licensed mental health professionals.

If you have reason to believe a child is, or has been, abused or neglected, Utah's general reporting duty applies to you. That is true even if:

- The client learned of the abuse from a religious leader
- A religious leader already knows
- The client asks you not to report

Situations involving adult clients who describe abuse from their own childhood, or who describe a perpetrator who may still have access to children, raise questions you should work through carefully. Confirm your obligations with your own counsel, your licensing board or your professional association. A lawyer the client consults works for the client, not for you.

## Clinical considerations

Clients who disclosed to a religious leader may carry layered harm: the abuse itself, and the response. Some common themes:

- **Shame tied to worthiness or standing.** A client may have been counseled to repent, forgive or keep quiet.
- **Confusion about confidentiality.** Clients may not know what a leader was allowed to keep private, and may assume therapy works the same way. Explaining the limits of your own confidentiality at the outset helps.
- **Divided loyalties.** Faith and community may remain central to the client's life. A legal consult does not require a client to leave their faith or speak against it.
- **Fear of exposure.** Clients may worry that family or congregation members will learn what they disclosed.

Meet the client where they are. Some will want information about legal options right away. Others will need time.

## When a legal consult may help

A consult may help when a client describes:

- Telling a religious leader, teacher or other adult about abuse as a child, with no apparent response
- Being discouraged from going to police or child protective services
- Abuse at a church activity, camp, youth program or other setting run by an organization
- A perpetrator whose conduct the organization knew about

Whether any claim exists depends on the facts, the people and organizations involved, and timing rules that vary by claim. Only a lawyer reviewing the specific situation can evaluate it.

## FAQ

### Does Utah's clergy exception mean a religious leader never has to report abuse?

No. The exception covers only a confession made directly by the perpetrator to a clergy member who is bound to keep it confidential. Information from other sources, including from a victim, must be reported under the statute.

### Does the clergy exception apply to therapists who work for a religious organization?

The exception applies to members of the clergy acting in a ministerial capacity. Clinicians should confirm their own obligations with counsel or their licensing board, especially if they hold both roles.

### Has Utah changed the clergy exception recently?

In 2024, H.B. 432 allowed clergy to report when they reasonably believe abuse is ongoing. Bills to remove the exception entirely, including H.B. 90 in 2020 and H.B. 115 in 2023, did not pass.

### My client told a church leader years ago. Is it too late to do anything?

Not necessarily. Timing rules depend on the claim, the defendant and the facts, and Utah has revisited its sexual-abuse limitation laws in recent years. The client should talk with a lawyer before assuming it is too late.

### Will talking to a lawyer require my client to go public or leave their faith?

No. A consult is confidential, and the client decides whether to take any next step.
