A client tells you about an assault. Then, almost in the same breath, they say something like: "But I can't report it. I'll get in trouble." If your client is, or was, a student at Brigham Young University, that fear has a history behind it.

This article is for Utah mental health professionals, including LCSWs, LMFTs, CMHCs and psychologists. It explains what happened at BYU around sexual-assault reporting and the Honor Code, what changed, and how you can talk with a client who is still afraid of school or ecclesiastical consequences. It also covers when a confidential legal consult may help.

It builds on the first article in this series, [A Therapist's Referral Guide: When a Utah Client's Trauma May Involve a Legal Claim](/resources/therapist-referral-guide-utah-trauma-legal-consult).

## What happened in 2016

In 2016, BYU students publicly reported that after they reported a sexual assault, the school's Honor Code Office opened investigations into their own conduct. Some described feeling treated like a target instead of a victim.

BYU formed an Advisory Council on Campus Response to Sexual Assault to study the problem. According to news coverage of the council's work, it found that the university's Title IX office had, in some cases, shared complainants' names and details with the Honor Code Office.

## What BYU changed

On October 26, 2016, BYU announced that it had accepted all 23 of the advisory council's recommendations. Among the changes:

- **An amnesty clause.** BYU said it would adopt an amnesty clause. As reported at the time, the principle is that "being a victim of sexual misconduct is never a violation of the Honor Code." The clause was formally added to university policy the following year.
- **Separate offices.** BYU committed to housing the Title IX office in a physical location separate from the Honor Code Office.
- **Limits on information sharing.** BYU said the Title IX office would not share information about a complainant with the Honor Code Office without the complainant's consent, unless the health or safety of others is at risk.

BYU's current Title IX materials describe amnesty and leniency. They say victims and witnesses who report "will not be disciplined by the university for any related Honor Code violations," with an exception when someone's health or safety is at risk. They also describe leniency for other Honor Code issues that come up during an investigation.

## The police-records issue

A separate issue involved BYU's police department.

A Utah Peace Officer Standards and Training (POST) investigation found that a former BYU police lieutenant had accessed protected police reports from the Provo City Police Department, the Orem City Police Department and the Utah County Sheriff's Office. Between August 2014 and June 2016, according to those findings, information from those reports was passed to BYU's Dean of Students Office, Title IX office and Honor Code Office. Prosecutors declined to file charges.

In February 2019, the Utah Department of Public Safety announced it would decertify BYU's police department. BYU appealed. In January 2021, an administrative judge dismissed the decertification effort, ruling that the department had met the minimum statutory requirements. The ruling allowed the department to keep operating. It did not erase the earlier POST findings about how reports had been shared.

Both parts of that story matter. The decertification did not stand, and the earlier findings still help explain why some survivors distrust campus reporting.

### Utah's 2019 law on private campus police

Also in 2019, the Utah Legislature passed S.B. 197, "Law Enforcement Agency Amendments." Among other things, it treats a certified police department of a private college or university as a law enforcement agency, and it brings law enforcement agencies within Utah's public records law (GRAMA) and the Governmental Immunity Act. News coverage at the time reported that the change subjected BYU's police department to state records requests. The law was not retroactive.

For clinicians, the practical point is simple. How a client's report moves between police, campus offices and others can depend on the agency, the timing and the law in effect then. A lawyer can sort that out.

## Why survivors may still be afraid

Policies have changed. Fear often has not. A client may worry about:

- **School discipline.** Even with amnesty, a client may fear that something else they did that night, such as drinking or being in a dorm after hours, will come up.
- **Loss of standing.** Scholarships, housing, employment on campus, or an ecclesiastical endorsement may feel at risk.
- **Family and church exposure.** Many students' families and religious leaders are closely connected to the school. A client may fear that a report will reach a bishop, a parent or a mission leader.
- **Being disbelieved.** If the client knows someone who had a bad experience reporting years ago, that story may shape their expectations today.
- **Old experiences.** A former student may have reported before 2016, or may have been told not to report. Those memories can carry shame and anger that never got processed.

None of these fears is irrational. They are rooted in real events and in tight-knit community structures.

## How to talk about it in session

You do not need to know every detail of BYU policy. You can be a steady, curious presence.

### Name the fear without arguing with it

"It sounds like part of what's hard is not knowing what would happen to you if you reported." Reflecting the fear often does more than reassuring the client that things have changed.

### Separate the decisions

Clients often merge several choices into one: telling a friend, getting medical care, reporting to police, reporting to Title IX, talking to a church leader, and talking to a lawyer. Help them see these as separate decisions they can make one at a time, or not at all.

### Respect the client's pace

Reporting is the client's decision. Your role is to make sure they have accurate information and feel free to choose.

### Be clear about your own limits

Clients may assume anything they tell you could reach the school or a church leader. Review your own confidentiality limits plainly, including any mandatory reporting duties that apply to you. Be clear that you will not share information with the school or ecclesiastical leaders without their written consent, except where the law requires it.

## When to suggest a legal consult

A legal consult may help when the client:

- reported and then faced discipline, an investigation of their own conduct, or another consequence;
- believes information about their report reached people they did not expect, such as an Honor Code office, an employer or a church leader;
- was discouraged from reporting, or was told to handle it internally;
- wants to understand options before deciding whether to report at all; or
- is wondering whether it is "too late" to do anything.

Deadlines vary by claim and by defendant, and some are short. Utah's legislature has revisited sexual-abuse limitation laws in recent sessions, including H.B. 117 in 2025. Claims involving public entities can require a formal notice of claim on a short timeline. An early consult protects options. It does not commit the client to anything.

## FAQ

### Does BYU still investigate students who report sexual assault for Honor Code violations?

BYU's current published policy describes amnesty for victims and witnesses for related Honor Code violations, with an exception when someone's health or safety is at risk, and leniency for other issues. A client with specific worries about their own situation should get answers from a lawyer, not from assumptions.

### Will the Title IX office tell the Honor Code Office about my client's report?

Since 2016, BYU has said its Title IX office does not share information about a complainant with the Honor Code Office without consent, except where health or safety is at risk or the person requests it. A lawyer can help a client understand how this applies to them.

### Was BYU's police department decertified?

The Utah Department of Public Safety announced decertification in February 2019. BYU appealed, and in January 2021 an administrative judge dismissed the effort. The department continued operating.

### My client reported years ago and was disciplined. Is there anything they can do now?

Possibly, but it depends on the facts, the claims and the deadlines that apply. The law has changed in recent years. The client should talk with a lawyer before assuming it is too late.

### Should I contact the school or the client's bishop on their behalf?

Not without your client's informed, written consent and a clear clinical reason, and not where it conflicts with your legal duties. Most of the time, your role is to support the client's own decisions.
