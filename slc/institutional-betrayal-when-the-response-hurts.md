Many survivors say something that surprises people who have not done this work: "The assault was terrible. But what happened after was worse."

They describe a school that seemed more worried about its reputation than about them. A program that punished them for speaking up. A leader who told them to keep it quiet. A records request that went nowhere.

Researchers have a name for this. It is called institutional betrayal. This article summarizes what the research shows, how it may show up in Utah therapy rooms, and when an institution's response can matter legally.

## What institutional betrayal means

Psychologists Carly Parnitzke Smith and Jennifer J. Freyd developed the concept from betrayal trauma theory. In their 2014 article in *American Psychologist*, they describe institutional betrayal as harm done by an institution to the people who depend on it. That includes failing to prevent wrongdoing within the institution, or failing to respond supportively when it happens, in settings where people reasonably expect protection.

The institution might be a school, a university, a youth program, a faith community, an employer, the military or a health system. What these settings share is dependence. People rely on them for education, belonging, housing, safety, a career or spiritual life. That dependence is part of what makes betrayal so painful.

### Acts of omission and commission

Smith and Freyd describe betrayal that can come from what an institution fails to do, and from what it actively does.

- **Omission:** weak prevention, poor screening or training, or an inadequate response to a report.
- **Commission:** covering up abuse, making reporting difficult, creating an environment where abuse seems normal, or punishing the person who reported.

They also note that betrayal can be isolated or systemic, and that what looks isolated at first may look systemic over time.

### Settings where risk may be higher

The 2014 article identifies institutional features that may raise the risk of betrayal. These include strict membership requirements, high prestige, priorities that put reputation ahead of members' well-being, rigid hierarchies, settings where "special" relationships excuse boundary-crossing, and patterns of institutional denial, such as blaming a single "bad apple" or questioning the person who reported.

None of these features means an institution has done anything wrong. They describe conditions under which betrayal may be more likely and harder to name.

## What the research found

### The 2013 study: "Dangerous Safe Havens"

In a 2013 study in the *Journal of Traumatic Stress*, Smith and Freyd surveyed 345 female undergraduates at a public university. They introduced the Institutional Betrayal Questionnaire, a checklist of ways an institution may have contributed to an unwanted sexual experience.

Among women who reported unwanted sexual experiences, 46% also reported at least one form of institutional betrayal. The authors reported that, among women who had experienced sexual assault, institutional betrayal was linked to higher levels of some trauma-related symptoms, such as anxiety and trauma-specific sexual symptoms.

The study had limits. It involved women at one university, it focused on sexual assault, and it was cross-sectional, so it cannot show cause and effect. But it put a measurable shape on something clinicians had long heard from clients.

### A growing body of work

Since then, researchers have studied institutional betrayal in many settings, including college campuses, high schools, workplaces, health care and the military. A 2024 scoping review in *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse* collected this work and helped map where the concept has been applied.

## Institutional courage

Freyd also introduced a counterpart idea: institutional courage. Her research group describes it as an institution's commitment to seek the truth and take moral action, even when that is costly or uncomfortable. It includes protecting and caring for the people who depend on the institution.

In the 2014 article, Smith and Freyd suggest steps that point toward courage, including honest self-study, treating reporting as an honorable act, protecting whistleblowers, training members to recognize and respond to abuse, and offering supportive responses to people who disclose.

Research on institutional courage is newer. A 2023 study in *PLOS ONE* surveyed employed adults who reported workplace sexual harassment. Institutional courage was associated with better workplace outcomes, and in some analyses it appeared to soften the negative associations of institutional betrayal. The study was cross-sectional, and its findings on psychological health were more limited, so it should be read as early, promising evidence rather than settled proof.

## How this may show up in Utah therapy rooms

Utah clients come from many communities: large public universities, private colleges, youth and residential programs, faith communities, sports and arts programs, and workplaces. Most of these institutions do a great deal of good. Institutional betrayal research is not an accusation against any of them. It is a tool for understanding why a client's distress may be tied as much to the response as to the original harm.

You may hear things like:

- "They investigated me instead of him."
- "Nobody ever followed up."
- "I lost my scholarship (or job, or calling, or housing) after I reported."
- "They told my parents or my leaders before I was ready."
- "They said they had no record of my report."
- "I still believe in the place. That is what makes it so hard."

That last one deserves attention. Because institutional betrayal involves something the client depends on, clients may feel loyalty and hurt at the same time. Smith and Freyd encourage clinicians to help clients explore and validate institutional betrayal, and to be a source of repair rather than a further betrayal.

## When an institution's response matters legally

Clinicians do not need to evaluate legal claims. But it helps to know that, legally, the response can matter, not just the original harm.

Depending on the facts, the setting and the law that applies, these kinds of issues can be relevant:

- **Failure to act.** The institution knew or had reason to know of a risk and did not respond reasonably.
- **Retaliation.** The client faced discipline, loss of a position or benefit, or other consequences after reporting. Some federal and state laws specifically address retaliation, including Title IX in education settings.
- **Records issues.** Reports are missing, incomplete, altered or not provided when requested.
- **Pressure or silencing.** The client was told to keep quiet, sign something, or handle it internally.
- **Information shared without consent.** Confidential information reached people the client did not expect.

None of this means a lawsuit is appropriate. Whether a claim exists depends on facts and law a lawyer needs to review. But these details often fade or disappear over time, which is one reason an early conversation can help.

### Timing matters

Deadlines vary by claim and by defendant, and some are short. When a public university, public school or government-run program is involved, Utah law generally requires a formal notice of claim before a lawsuit, on its own timeline. Clients should not assume they have plenty of time.

## FAQ

### Is institutional betrayal a clinical diagnosis?

No. It is a research concept describing harm done by an institution to people who depend on it. It can help explain a client's symptoms and experience, but it is not a diagnosis.

### Does institutional betrayal mean the institution acted illegally?

Not necessarily. The research concept is broader than any legal standard. Some betrayal experiences may also raise legal issues, such as retaliation or failure to act, and a lawyer can help sort that out.

### What is institutional courage?

It is the counterpart to institutional betrayal: an institution's commitment to seek the truth and take moral action, including protecting and caring for the people who depend on it. Early research suggests it may soften some harms associated with betrayal.

### How can I talk about this without damaging my client's relationship with their community?

Let the client lead. Naming the institution's response as a separate source of harm can validate the client's experience without asking them to reject their school, faith or community.

### When should I mention a legal consult?

When the client raises the institution's response, retaliation, missing records or pressure to stay silent, or when a public entity is involved. Offer it as an option, never as an expectation.
