Prince was one of the most famous musicians in the world. When he died in 2016, he left no will. What followed was about six years of court proceedings, a fight with the IRS over what his estate was worth, and, according to the Associated Press, tens of millions of dollars paid to lawyers and consultants.

You don't need a music catalog for the same thing to happen to your family on a smaller scale. Here's what happened, and what it means for you.

## What happened to Prince's estate

- **No will.** Because Prince left no will, state law, not Prince, decided who inherited. His heirs were his siblings.
- **A bank ran the estate.** A corporate administrator, Comerica Bank & Trust, ended up managing his affairs, not someone he chose in writing.
- **A fight over value.** The administrator first valued the estate at about $82.3 million. The IRS said it was worth about $163.2 million. In early 2022 they agreed on $156.4 million, and the IRS dropped a $6.4 million penalty as part of the settlement.
- **Years of waiting.** It took roughly six years before distributions could begin. Two of the sibling heirs died before the estate was settled.
- **The money went in directions no one planned.** Some heirs sold interests along the way. By the end, a New York music company, Primary Wave, was set to receive roughly half the estate, according to the AP.

None of that reflects what Prince wanted, because he never wrote down what he wanted.

## Why this matters for normal families

Most families will never owe federal estate tax. But the other problems in Prince's story are common:

1. **The wrong people can end up in charge.** Without a will, a court decides who manages your estate, and it may not be the person you would have picked.
2. **State law decides who inherits.** The default rules may not match your wishes, especially in blended families, for unmarried partners, or for stepchildren.
3. **Probate takes time and money.** Even a modest estate can sit in court for months while bills, the house and accounts wait.
4. **Your kids' guardian isn't automatic.** If you have minor children, a will is where you name who raises them.
5. **Family conflict fills the gap.** When there's no written plan, people argue about what you "would have wanted."

## What a simple plan would have changed

A basic estate plan answers the questions Prince's estate spent years fighting over:

- **Who gets what.** A will or a living trust states it in writing.
- **Who's in charge.** You name your own executor or trustee.
- **Who raises your kids.** You name a guardian.
- **Who makes decisions if you can't.** A power of attorney and a health care directive cover that while you're alive.
- **Avoiding probate.** A funded living trust can keep many assets out of court entirely.

For most families, this isn't complicated. It's a few decisions and a signing appointment.
